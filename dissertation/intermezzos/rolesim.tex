\textit{\large Where \RoleBox{} maps what actors \emph{say} and \emph{do},\\[0.3em]
\RoleSim{} classifies what they \emph{are} in relational structure.}
\end{center}

\vspace{1.5cm}

\begin{chapteroverview}
This intermezzo introduces \RoleSim, an influence typology tool that classifies
actors by their structural position in networks derived from \RoleBox{} data.
Applied to the EIT Twitter mention network (497~actors, 6,075~edges) and the
Crunchbase venture similarity network (3,000~ventures, 2,545~edges),
\RoleSim{} reveals starkly different role distributions across modalities---offering
an independent, network-structural lens on the role constellations mapped in
Chapters~\ref{ch:rolefield}--\ref{ch:power}.
\end{chapteroverview}


%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section*{From Text to Network Roles}
%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The empirical chapters extract role signals from text: website self-descriptions
(Chapter~\ref{ch:interstitial}), tweet content and co-mention patterns
(Chapter~\ref{ch:rolefield}), venture descriptions and embeddings
(Chapter~\ref{ch:venture}), visual registers (Chapter~\ref{ch:visual}), and
news narratives (Chapter~\ref{ch:power}). Each modality captures
\emph{what actors say or do}---declared roles, claimed identities,
attributed actions.

But roles are also relational positions.\sidenote{\textbf{Structural vs.\
declared roles}---An actor may claim to be a ``hub'' on its website while
occupying a peripheral position in the actual interaction network. Comparing
declared and structural roles operationalizes the claim--action gap central
to this dissertation.}
An actor's role in a network depends not on what it says about itself but on
where it sits in the flow of information, resources, and attention. \RoleSim{}
addresses this complementary question: given the networks that \RoleBox{} data
produce, what \emph{structural} roles do actors occupy?


%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section*{The Influence Typology}
%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\RoleSim{} implements an influence typology algorithm adapted from the
\texttt{watch-complexity} library,\sidenote{Based on the
\texttt{watch-complexity} library by Davide Miceli
(\url{github.com/davidemiceli/watch-complexity}). The JavaScript implementation
was ported to Python and extended with six innovation-ecosystem role types.
Code: \texttt{rolesim/src/rolesim/influence/typology.py}}
which classifies nodes based on directed information and resource flow patterns.
The algorithm computes a normalized influence score (0--100) for each node and
assigns a role type based on degree ratios, betweenness centrality, and flow
characteristics.

The typology defines 22~role types organized into six functional
categories:\sidenote{\textbf{22~types}---16 from the original
\texttt{watch-complexity} library (based on communication and network theory)
plus 6 extensions for innovation ecosystem contexts: Core Coordinator,
Boundary Spanner, Knowledge Broker, Resource Mobilizer, Legitimator,
and Niche Actor.}

\tableminimal
\begin{center}
\scriptsize
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{2.8cm}p{4.5cm}p{4.5cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Category} & \textbf{Role Types} & \textbf{Intermediary Interpretation} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Central positions} & Hub, Tophub, Bridge &
  Core coordinators, boundary spanners between clusters \\
\addlinespace
\textbf{Amplification} & Amplifier, Megamplifier, Vulcano &
  Knowledge diffusers, accelerators, research producers \\
\addlinespace
\textbf{Flow control} & Reducer, Dam, Channeler &
  Gatekeepers, policy filters, grant distributors \\
\addlinespace
\textbf{Exchange} & Transceiver, Connector, Chain &
  Bilateral partners, internal coordinators \\
\addlinespace
\textbf{Terminal} & Emitter, Receiver, Blackhole, Low Emitter,
  Emitter Branch, Receiver Branch &
  Sources, sinks, and peripheral actors \\
\addlinespace
\textbf{Ecosystem} & Core Coordinator, Boundary Spanner,
  Knowledge Broker, Resource Mobilizer, Legitimator, Niche Actor &
  Extended types for innovation intermediary contexts \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\captionof{table}[Influence typology categories]{The 22~influence types
organized by functional category. For practical analysis, related types are
collapsed into the six categories shown.}
\labtab{influence-typology}
\end{center}
\normalsize

Crucially, roles are \emph{computed} from network position, not assigned
exogenously---operationalizing the CAS principle (Chapter~\ref{ch:cas}) that
roles emerge from relational structure rather than being designed top-down.


%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section*{Application to RoleBox Networks}
%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The \RoleSim{} pipeline was applied to two networks constructed from
\RoleBox{} data:\sidenote{Pipeline run: January 2026.
Code: \texttt{rolesim/scripts/generate\_rolesim\_data.py}.
A third network (visual, 30~nodes) produced no edges and was excluded.}

\subsection*{Social Network: Twitter Mentions}

The EIT Twitter mention network captures \emph{who mentions whom} among
497~actors identified in Chapter~\ref{ch:rolefield}.
The network is directed (mentions are asymmetric) with 6,075~weighted edges.

\begin{center}
\scriptsize
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrp{5cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Role} & \textbf{Count} & \textbf{\%} & \textbf{Interpretation} \\
\midrule
Receiver   & 231 & 46.5 & Actors primarily mentioned \emph{by} others---policy
  figures, EU institutions, prominent individuals who attract attention
  without reciprocating \\
\addlinespace
Emitter    & 215 & 43.3 & Actors who mention others but are rarely mentioned
  back---active commentators, smaller organizations seeking visibility \\
\addlinespace
Transceiver & 24 &  4.8 & Balanced in/out flow---bilateral partners, KIC
  offices with reciprocal communication patterns \\
\addlinespace
Amplifier  &  16 &  3.2 & High out-degree relative to in-degree---knowledge
  diffusers, media accounts, event promoters \\
\addlinespace
Absorber   &  10 &  2.0 & Maximum absorption, minimal output---data
  collection accounts, monitoring bots \\
\addlinespace
Hub        &   1 &  0.2 & Highest centrality---the single dominant
  orchestrator in the mention network \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\captionof{table}[Social network role distribution]{Influence typology applied
to the EIT Twitter mention network ($n = 497$ actors, 6,075~directed edges).
The distribution is heavily polarized: nearly 90\% of actors are either pure
Receivers or pure Emitters.}
\labtab{rolesim-social}
\end{center}

The striking finding is the network's \textbf{polarization}: 89.8\% of actors
are either pure Receivers (mentioned but not mentioning) or pure Emitters
(mentioning but not mentioned). Only 8.2\% occupy exchange or amplification
roles. A single Hub coordinates the entire field. This mirrors the
``broadcast'' communication pattern typical of institutional Twitter
ecosystems---many actors talk \emph{at} prominent figures rather than
\emph{with} each other.

\subsection*{Crunchbase Network: Venture Similarity}

The Crunchbase network connects 3,000~EIT-sector ventures through
description embedding similarity (Chapter~\ref{ch:venture}).
The network is undirected with 2,545~edges, where edges represent ventures
whose textual descriptions are semantically close.

\begin{center}
\scriptsize
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrp{5cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Role} & \textbf{Count} & \textbf{\%} & \textbf{Interpretation} \\
\midrule
Transceiver & 1,699 & 56.6 & Balanced similarity connections---ventures
  positioned in dense, reciprocal clusters \\
\addlinespace
Emitter     &   530 & 17.7 & Ventures similar to many others but not
  reciprocally matched---generic descriptions \\
\addlinespace
Receiver    &   517 & 17.2 & Ventures that others resemble but that
  themselves are distinctive---niche leaders \\
\addlinespace
Absorber    &   117 &  3.9 & Highly generic ventures absorbing similarity
  from many directions \\
\addlinespace
Amplifier   &   113 &  3.8 & Ventures whose descriptions ``radiate''
  similarity---sector-defining language \\
\addlinespace
Bridge      &    18 &  0.6 & Cross-sector connectors linking distinct
  venture clusters \\
\addlinespace
Hub         &     6 &  0.2 & Central ventures with highest overall
  similarity connectivity \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\captionof{table}[Crunchbase network role distribution]{Influence typology
applied to the EIT venture similarity network ($n = 3{,}000$ ventures,
2,545~undirected edges). The distribution is dominated by Transceivers---most
ventures occupy reciprocal positions in similarity space.}
\labtab{rolesim-crunchbase}
\end{center}

The Crunchbase network shows a fundamentally different structure: 56.6\%
Transceivers (balanced exchange) versus the social network's 4.8\%.
This reflects the nature of similarity networks---semantic proximity is
inherently more symmetric than mention behavior. The 18~Bridges and
6~Hubs mark ventures that span KIC sector boundaries, connecting
otherwise distinct clusters (e.g., climate and digital, health and food).


%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section*{Cross-Modal Role Comparison}
%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Comparing role distributions across the two networks reveals how
\emph{the same ecosystem looks different depending on which relational
dimension you examine}:\sidenote{\textbf{Modality shapes structure}---The
social network captures attention and communication; the similarity network
captures positioning and competitive proximity. Different relational
dimensions produce different role structures from the same ecosystem.}

\begin{center}
\scriptsize
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Role Category} & \textbf{Social (\%)} & \textbf{Crunchbase (\%)} \\
\midrule
Central (Hub/Bridge)     &  0.2 &  0.8 \\
Amplification            &  3.2 &  3.8 \\
Exchange (Transceiver)   &  4.8 & 56.6 \\
Terminal (Emitter/Receiver/Absorber) & 91.8 & 38.8 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\captionof{table}[Cross-modal role comparison]{Role category distribution
across modalities. The social network is polarized (92\% terminal roles);
the similarity network is balanced (57\% exchange roles).}
\labtab{rolesim-comparison}
\end{center}

This divergence is itself a finding: the EIT ecosystem's \emph{communication}
structure is hierarchical and broadcast-oriented, while its
\emph{positioning} structure is distributed and reciprocal. Actors who are
peripheral in the mention network may occupy central positions in the
similarity landscape, and vice versa. The claim--action gap identified in
Chapter~\ref{ch:interstitial} (what actors say vs.\ what they do) extends
to a \emph{structure--structure gap}: different relational dimensions
of the same ecosystem yield different role assignments.

\section*{Knowledge Graph Integration}
%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Beyond dyadic networks, \RoleSim{} constructs a knowledge graph that
integrates organizational attributes with relational
structure.\sidenote{Knowledge graph: 11,927~nodes and 33,438~edges
linking companies to sectors, countries, and clusters.
Code: \texttt{rolesim/scripts/generate\_kg\_embeddings.py}}
The graph connects 11,863~companies to 8~KIC sectors, 31~countries, and
25~embedding clusters through typed edges (sector membership, geographic
location, cluster assignment, and semantic similarity). This multi-relational
representation enables queries that cross modality boundaries---for example,
identifying which country--sector combinations produce the most Bridge actors,
or whether Hub ventures cluster geographically.

\section*{Limitations and Future Directions}
%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\RoleSim{} in its current form provides \textbf{static classification},
not dynamic simulation.\sidenote{\textbf{Honest scoping}---The influence
typology classifies actors at a single point in time. The agent-based
simulation framework exists as a proof-of-concept on synthetic data but
does not yet produce emergent role dynamics on real networks. Extending
the simulation to real data is future work.}
Several limitations should be noted:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0.5cm, itemsep=0.3em]
\item[Static snapshot] The typology classifies actors at one point in time.
  Role \emph{dynamics}---how actors move between positions over months or
  years---require longitudinal network data that the current pipeline does
  not produce.

\item[Collapsed categories] The pipeline output uses six collapsed categories
  (Hub, Bridge, Amplifier, Transceiver, Emitter, Receiver/Absorber) rather
  than the full 22-type resolution. Finer-grained classification requires
  denser networks than the current data provide.

\item[Network construction choices] Role classifications depend on how
  networks are constructed---similarity thresholds, mention counting
  methods, edge weighting. Different construction choices would yield
  different role distributions.

\item[No ground truth] There is no external validation of whether the
  typology assignments are ``correct.'' Face validity is encouraging
  (the single Hub in the social network is the most central EIT account;
  Bridges in Crunchbase span recognizable sector boundaries), but
  systematic validation remains future work.
\end{description}

An agent-based simulation framework (\texttt{rolesim/src/rolesim/simulation/})
implements the CAS mechanisms from Chapter~\ref{ch:cas}---emergence,
self-organization, co-evolution, feedback loops---as computational rules.
Proof-of-concept runs on synthetic networks (50~nodes) demonstrate that
role differentiation and hub emergence \emph{can} arise from local agent
interactions without central coordination. Extending these dynamics to the
real networks presented here---enabling roles to emerge, shift, and
stabilize over simulated time---is a priority for future development.

\section*{Summary}
%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\begin{summarybox}
This intermezzo introduced \RoleSim, an influence typology tool that
classifies actors by structural network position. Applied to two \RoleBox{}
networks, it reveals contrasting role structures: the Twitter mention network
is polarized (90\% terminal roles, one Hub), while the Crunchbase similarity
network is distributed (57\% exchange roles, 18~Bridges).

The cross-modal comparison demonstrates that role assignments depend on
which relational dimension is examined---communication versus positioning
yield different structural portraits of the same ecosystem. This
\emph{structure--structure gap} complements the claim--action gap identified
in the empirical chapters, reinforcing the dissertation's central argument
that multimodal analysis is necessary because no single perspective captures
the full constellation.

The influence typology provides a reusable \textbf{DSR artifact}: a
computational workflow (Curation~$\to$ network construction~$\to$
typology classification~$\to$ role distribution) that can be applied
to any network derived from textual data. As such, it instantiates
Meta-Design Requirement~1 (systematic data transformation) and
contributes to the reproducible toolkit that this dissertation develops.
\end{summarybox}
