\section{Introduction}
\labsec{synthesis-intro}
%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The fundamental question driving this dissertation is: \emph{How should data-driven, theory-guided workflows be designed to enable multimodal cartography of interstitial role constellations in sustainability transitions?}\sidenote{\iconidea\ \textbf{Integration challenge}---Each modality reveals different facets of the same organizations. Synthesis requires both combining and contrasting these views.} The preceding empirical chapters demonstrated these workflows from different perspectives:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.3em]
\item Chapter~\ref{ch:interstitial} revealed \textbf{claimed roles}---what organizations say about themselves through website positioning and vocabulary
\item Chapter~\ref{ch:rolefield} revealed \textbf{relational roles}---how organizations position themselves in social media networks and discourse
\item Chapter~\ref{ch:venture} revealed \textbf{resourced roles}---what portfolio compositions and investment patterns reveal about enacted functions
\item Chapter~\ref{ch:visual} revealed \textbf{embodied roles}---how visual registers construct and communicate institutional identity
\item Chapter~\ref{ch:power} revealed \textbf{power dynamics}---how discourse constructs agency hierarchies among ecosystem actors
\end{itemize}

Each modality uncovered distinct role constellation patterns across the EIT ecosystem. But the question remains: \emph{How do these views combine to reveal the complete role profile of each interstitial actor?}

This synthesis chapter addresses three integrative questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Integration:} How do signals from different modalities converge or diverge, and what do these patterns tell us about role dynamics?
    \item \textbf{Theory:} What theoretical contributions emerge from multimodal role constellation cartography?
    \item \textbf{Design:} What design knowledge do the workflows produce, and how can they break down open science barriers while building up cumulative research infrastructure?
    \item \textbf{Practice:} What are the implications for transition governance and innovation policy?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Cross-Modal Triangulation}[Triangulation]
\labsec{triangulation}
%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The central methodological challenge of multimodal cartography is integration: how do we synthesize signals from five different data modalities into coherent role profiles?\sidenote{\textbf{Triangulation logic}---Classic triangulation seeks convergence \cite{denzin1970triangulation,jick1979mixing}. Multimodal cartography goes further: divergence is equally informative.} Our approach treats both convergence and divergence as informative.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0.5cm, itemsep=0.3em]
\item[Claimed Roles (Websites)] How organizations present themselves---their aspirational sociotype and strategic positioning.
\item[Attributed Roles (News)] How external observers characterize organizations---their public reputation and legitimacy.
\item[Resourced Roles (Investment)] Which roles receive financial backing---the material basis of role enactment.
\item[Relational Roles (Social Media)] How organizations position themselves in network discourse---their relational sociotype.
\item[Embodied Roles (Visual)] How roles are materially performed through imagery---the visual semiotics of innovation.
\end{description}

These views may \textbf{converge} (reinforcing confidence in role attributions) or \textbf{diverge} (revealing tensions, aspirations, or strategic positioning). Both patterns carry analytical signal.

\subsection{Convergence as Validation}

When multiple modalities agree on an actor's role profile, we gain confidence in the attribution:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0.5cm, itemsep=0.3em]
\item[Role Confirmation] An actor claiming ``connector'' on their website, positioned centrally in social networks, and described as ``facilitator'' in news coverage---this triangulated convergence validates the role attribution with high confidence.\sidenote{\textbf{High-confidence roles}---When three or more modalities agree, false positives become unlikely. We weight these attributions most heavily.}

\item[Cross-Modal Correlation] Computing correlation coefficients between role scores derived from different modalities reveals which role categories are robustly measurable across contexts.

\item[Triangulation Strength] The more modalities that agree, the stronger the evidence for a role attribution. We weight integrated role scores accordingly.
\end{description}

\subsection{Divergence as Signal}

When modalities disagree, we have not encountered noise---we have found something analytically interesting:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0.5cm, itemsep=0.3em]
\item[Aspiration Gaps] When claimed roles (websites) exceed enacted roles (network positions), we observe aspirational positioning---organizations projecting future selves.\sidenote{\textbf{Aspiration as strategy}---Claiming roles before fully occupying them can be strategic: signaling intent, attracting partners, building toward future positions.}

\item[Perception Gaps] When attributed roles (news) differ from claimed roles, we observe legitimacy contests---struggles over organizational identity.

\item[Resource Gaps] When claimed roles lack corresponding investment support, we observe unfunded ambitions---strategic intentions without material backing.

\item[Strategic Gaps] Some divergence is deliberate---organizations may claim certain roles externally while enacting others operationally.
\end{description}

\begin{insightbox}[The Connector Gap]
34\% of organizations in our sample claim connector roles on their websites, but only 13\% occupy bridging positions in network analysis. This 21-percentage-point gap is not measurement error---it reveals the prevalence of intermediary \textit{aspiration} exceeding intermediary \textit{reality} in sustainability transitions.
\end{insightbox}

\subsection{Why Modalities Diverge: A Sociomaterial Account}

The connector gap and the broader patterns of cross-modal divergence demand theoretical explanation. Why should different data modalities produce systematically different role profiles for the same organizations? Three competing accounts present themselves.\sidenote{\textbf{Three explanations}---Measurement error, strategic behavior, and sociomaterial constitution offer increasingly sophisticated accounts of why modalities diverge.}

\paragraph{Measurement error.} The simplest explanation treats divergence as noise---different methods introduce different biases, and the ``true'' role profile lies somewhere in the averaged signal. This account implies that better instruments would yield convergence. But the connector gap is too systematic and too large (21 percentage points, reproduced across all eight KIC domains) to be dismissed as noise. Measurement error would produce random rather than directional divergence.

\paragraph{Strategic behaviour.} A more sophisticated account holds that organizations deliberately present different faces to different audiences---claiming connector roles on websites to attract partners while enacting narrower functions in practice. This is the implicit explanation running through Chapters~\ref{ch:interstitial}--\ref{ch:power}. It captures something real: organizations do engage in impression management \cite{gioia2013organizational}. But it treats the digital platforms through which roles are performed as neutral transmission channels and leaves unanswered the question of \emph{why} certain roles are more claimable on certain platforms.

\paragraph{Sociomaterial constitution.} The Information Systems perspective we adopt here offers a third account, grounded in the ``material turn'' that has reshaped IS scholarship over the past two decades \cite{Orlikowski2007,orlikowski2008sociomateriality,leonardi2012materiality}. On this view, cross-modal divergence is not primarily methodological (noise) or behavioural (strategy) but \emph{ontological}: different digital platforms \emph{materially co-constitute} different facets of organizational roles through their specific affordances, constraints, and architectures.\sidenote{\textbf{Constitutive entanglement}---\textcite{Orlikowski2007}: ``The social and the material are constitutively entangled in everyday life.'' Platforms do not merely record roles; they participate in producing them.}

Following \textcite{orlikowski2008sociomateriality} and \textcite{leonardi2013theoretical}, we argue that roles are \textbf{multiply constituted} through different sociomaterial assemblages:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0.5cm, itemsep=0.3em]
\item[Websites $\rightarrow$ Claimed roles] constitute organizational identity through editorial control affordances. Content management systems, navigation hierarchies, and page templates shape \emph{what kinds of role claims become articulable}. The Knowledge Triangle's neat tripartition into education, research, and business is partly an artifact of how websites organize information into discrete sections---a material affordance that rewards categorical clarity over the messy hybridity of actual practice.

\item[Twitter/X $\rightarrow$ Relational roles] constitute field positions through network architecture affordances. Lists, follows, retweets, and mentions create classification and interaction patterns that are \emph{platform-specific social facts} \cite{scott2014entanglements}. The co-classification methodology of Chapter~\ref{ch:rolefield} depends entirely on a material feature---Twitter lists---that makes certain kinds of grouping possible while foreclosing others.

\item[Crunchbase $\rightarrow$ Resourced roles] constitute enacted functions through investment database schemas. Standardized funding categories, industry tags, and investment round records make certain role dimensions legible (financial, sectoral) while rendering others invisible (relational, discursive). As \textcite{kallinikos2013ambivalent} argue, digital artifacts carry an ``ambivalent ontology''---they simultaneously represent and constitute the phenomena they encode.

\item[News media $\rightarrow$ Attributed roles] constitute public identities through editorial gatekeeping conventions. Journalistic norms of newsworthiness, balance, and narrative structure shape which actors appear, in which roles, and with what valence. The connotation frames analysed in Chapter~\ref{ch:power} are not neutral measurements of power but products of how news discourse \emph{materially produces} attribution through verb selection, subject positioning, and source citation practices.

\item[Visual materials $\rightarrow$ Embodied roles] constitute institutional identity through semiotic communication affordances. The visual registers analysed in Chapter~\ref{ch:visual} are themselves material accomplishments---produced through photographic conventions, graphic design tools, and platform display requirements that shape what kinds of institutional identities become \emph{visually enactable}.
\end{description}

\begin{insightbox}[The Sociomaterial Reframing]
The connector gap (34\% claimed vs.\ 13\% enacted) is not a case of organizations ``lying'' on their websites. It is a case of two different platform materialities producing two different---both real---role realities. Websites \emph{materially afford} connector claims through editorial self-description; network analysis \emph{materially affords} structural position measurement through interaction data. The 21-point gap between them is itself a sociomaterial phenomenon: it maps the space between the role worlds that different platforms create.
\end{insightbox}

This sociomaterial account has direct implications for how we understand the dissertation's core DSR artifacts. The \RoleBox{} computational workflows, datasets, and design requirements are not neutral instruments operating on pre-given data. They are themselves \textbf{sociomaterial assemblages} that create new observation capacities: each pipeline transforms platform-specific digital traces through computational infrastructure (APIs, embedding models, network algorithms) into design knowledge about organizational roles.\sidenote{\textbf{Reflexive grounding}---The same sociomaterial logic applies to our own research infrastructure. \RoleBox{} does not merely \emph{discover} role constellations; it \emph{co-produces} them through its specific material configurations.} The Meta-Design Requirements (MDR-1 through MDR-4) are implicitly sociomaterial: they specify how material dimensions (data modalities, computational techniques) and social dimensions (theoretical constructs, interpretive frameworks) must be reconciled through designed artifacts.

This reflexive move positions Information Systems not merely as the disciplinary origin of Design Science Research but as the \textbf{metatheoretical paradigm} that grounds the entire dissertation: DSR supplies the methodology for artifact construction; sociomateriality supplies the ontology that explains why multimodal cartography works and why cross-modal divergence is analytically productive rather than merely inconvenient.

The entanglement runs deeper still. As \textcite{owoyele2025datacentering} argue, sustainability transitions and digital transformation are themselves constitutively entangled: the data centres, cloud platforms, and digital infrastructures through which we study role constellations consume more electricity than many nations and are simultaneously infrastructure \emph{for} transitions (enabling smart grids, precision agriculture, circular economy platforms) and infrastructure \emph{requiring} transition (decarbonisation, circularity, justice). Studying transitions \emph{through} digital infrastructure that itself \emph{requires} transition is the distinctive reflexive condition of contemporary computational social science---and IS provides the metatheoretical vocabulary to theorise it \cite{cecezkecmanovic2014sociomateriality,plantin2018infrastructure}.

\subsection{Integration Methods}

We employ three quantitative approaches to cross-modal integration:

\paragraph{Weighted Aggregation.} Combine modality-specific role scores with weights reflecting data quality and theoretical relevance:\sidenote{\textbf{Weight choices}---We weight enacted roles (networks, investment) higher than claimed roles (websites), as behavior is harder to fake than self-presentation.}
$$R_{\text{integrated}} = \sum_{m} w_m \cdot R_m$$
where $w_m$ weights each modality $m$ and $R_m$ represents the role score from that modality.

\paragraph{Profile Vectors.} Represent each actor as a high-dimensional vector across modalities and role dimensions:
$$\mathbf{v}_{\text{actor}} = [R_1^{\text{web}}, R_1^{\text{news}}, \ldots, R_k^{\text{visual}}]$$
Actor similarity is computed via cosine distance, enabling clustering and typology development.

\paragraph{Gap Metrics.} Quantify divergence between specific modality pairs:
$$\text{Gap}_{ij} = |R_i - R_j|$$
High gaps flag actors for qualitative investigation of role tensions.

\section{Cross-Chapter Integration}[Integration]
\labsec{cross-chapter}
%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Comparing findings across the empirical chapters reveals systematic patterns in how role constellations manifest across intermediation domains.\sidenote{\icondata\ \textbf{8 KICs, 5 modalities}---Cross-chapter integration draws on 40 KIC-modality combinations, enabling robust pattern identification.}

\subsection{Domain-Specific Role Configurations}

The eight EIT KIC sectors exhibit distinct role constellation patterns:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0.5cm, itemsep=0.3em]
\item[Climate and Energy] Dominated by large incumbent partners (utilities, research institutes) with established legitimacy.\sidenote{\textbf{Incumbent integration}---Climate-KIC and InnoEnergy show how established players can participate in transitions while maintaining regime positions.} Role constellations show hub-and-spoke patterns with KICs as central coordinators. High convergence between claimed and attributed roles---these actors are who they say they are.

\item[Health and Digital] More distributed constellations with numerous startup actors.\sidenote{\textbf{Startup-driven sectors}---EIT Health and Digital show higher churn, more aspirational positioning, and faster role evolution.} Greater divergence between claimed roles (ambitious positioning) and resourced roles (selective investment). Faster role evolution as technology cycles accelerate.

\item[Food and Raw Materials] Hybrid patterns combining research anchors with industry incumbents. Geographic clustering matters more---regional ecosystems shape role possibilities. Slower constellation dynamics reflecting longer innovation cycles.

\item[Urban Mobility and Manufacturing] Newest KICs show less crystallized role structures.\sidenote{\textbf{Nascent constellations}---The youngest KICs (2019) offer a natural experiment in constellation formation.} More aspirational claiming, less established attribution. Opportunity for intentional constellation design.
\end{description}

\subsection{Cross-Domain Patterns}

Despite domain differences, several patterns recur:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The knowledge triangle imbalance persists.} Across all domains, business vocabulary (49\%) dominates over research (37\%) and education (14\%).\sidenote{\textbf{EIT's founding vision}---The 2008 EIT Regulation envisioned equal integration of business, research, and education. Reality diverges.} The EIT's founding vision of balanced integration remains unrealized in organizational self-presentation.

\section{Theoretical Contributions}[Theory]
\labsec{theoretical-contributions}
%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

This dissertation makes several theoretical contributions to the study of roles in sustainability transitions:

\subsection{Role Constellations as Complex Adaptive Systems}

The CAS reconceptualization (Chapter~\ref{ch:cas}) provides mechanisms---emergence, self-organization, co-evolution, non-linearity, feedback loops---that explain observed constellation dynamics while enabling computational operationalization \sidecite{holland1995hidden,kauffman1993origins}.\sidenote{\textbf{Theory-method bridge}---CAS mechanisms are simultaneously theoretically meaningful and computationally tractable---enabling the bridge this dissertation builds.} This addresses a persistent gap between transitions theory (which emphasizes complexity) and transitions methods (which often assume linearity).

Key theoretical insights include:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.3em]
\item \textbf{Emergence is real.} Constellation-level properties (functional coverage, structural resilience, adaptive capacity) cannot be reduced to individual actor attributes. The whole exceeds the sum of parts.\sidenote{\textbf{Irreducibility}---You cannot predict constellation behavior from actor profiles alone. Structure matters.}

\item \textbf{Self-organization produces structure.} Without central coordination, role differentiation and niche formation occur through local interactions. KICs do not orchestrate their ecosystems---they participate in them.

\item \textbf{Co-evolution couples actors and context.} Role possibilities depend on what others do. Success by one actor reshapes opportunities for others. Landscape pressures ripple through constellations unevenly.

\item \textbf{Non-linearity means surprises.} Small triggers can produce large effects (and vice versa). Tipping points and phase transitions are possible---though predicting them remains difficult.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Multimodal Identity Construction}

Organizational roles are not unitary---they are constructed differently across channels and audiences \cite{albert1985organizational,gioia2013organizational}.\sidenote{\textbf{Goffman meets transitions}---Organizations, like individuals, present different ``selves'' in different contexts. Multimodal analysis captures this multiplicity.} This exploratory analysis reveals patterns suggesting that:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.3em]
\item \textbf{Identity is perspectival.} What an organization ``is'' depends on who is looking and through what lens.

\item \textbf{Gaps are strategic.} Divergence between claimed and enacted roles is not failure but positioning---creating space for identity evolution.

\item \textbf{Legitimacy is multimodal.} Organizations must construct coherent identities across channels, but coherence need not mean identity---strategic ambiguity can serve multiple audiences.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Cartographic Metaphor}

Treating role analysis as cartography---mapping rather than measuring---reframes methodological assumptions:\sidenote{\textbf{Cartographic turn}---The shift from ``measurement'' to ``mapping'' carries epistemological implications: acknowledging partiality, construction, and purpose.}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.3em]
\item \textbf{Maps are partial.} No single map captures all terrain. Multiple projections reveal different features.

\item \textbf{Maps are constructed.} Cartographic choices (what to include, how to represent) shape what becomes visible.

\item \textbf{Maps enable navigation.} The goal is not perfect representation but useful orientation for action.
\end{itemize}

\section{Methodological Contributions}[Methods]
\labsec{methodological-contributions}
%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The dissertation advances methodology for studying innovation ecosystems in several ways:

\subsection{The RoleBox Framework}

\RoleBox{} provides an integrated computational infrastructure for multimodal role constellation analysis.\sidenote{\textbf{Open source}---All \RoleBox{} code is available at \texttt{github.com/babajideowoyele/rolebox} for replication and extension.} Its contributions include:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0.5cm, itemsep=0.3em]
\item[Modular Architecture] Six data modality pipelines (Wikipedia, websites, news, Crunchbase, social media, visual) can be used independently or in combination.

\item[Reproducible Workflows] Documented pipelines enable replication and adaptation. Others can apply these methods to different ecosystems.

\item[Open Infrastructure] Code, data schemas, and analytical procedures are publicly available for critique, extension, and reuse.
\end{description}

\subsection{Multimodal Data Integration}

Combining five data modalities required developing integration protocols that:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.3em]
\item Align entity identities across sources (the same organization has different names/identifiers in different databases)
\item Handle missing data (not all organizations appear in all modalities)
\item Weight signals by reliability and relevance
\item Interpret divergence as signal rather than noise
\end{itemize}

These protocols transfer to other multimodal research contexts.

\subsection{Computational Role Extraction}

The dissertation demonstrates that NLP techniques can extract role-relevant information from unstructured text at scale:\sidenote{\textbf{Computational extraction}---NLP enables analysis at scales impossible manually. But human judgment remains essential for validation and interpretation.}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.3em]
\item \textbf{Role vocabulary identification} through embedding-based semantic clustering
\item \textbf{Sociotype detection} through community detection on role co-occurrence networks
\item \textbf{Semantic role labeling} for extracting actor-action-target triplets
\end{itemize}

These techniques complement rather than replace qualitative analysis---they surface patterns that warrant deeper investigation.

\section{Bridging Data-Driven and Theory-Driven Research}[Bridging Paradigms]
\labsec{bridging-paradigms}
%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Maass et al. \citeyear{maass2018data} identify fundamental challenges at the intersection of data-driven and theory-driven research. This dissertation's contribution lies precisely at this intersection: we bring \emph{multimodal organic data} (data-driven) to bear on \emph{role constellation theory} (theory-driven). This section articulates the challenges we faced and how multimodal cartography addresses them.\sidenote{\textbf{Two solitudes}---Data science and domain theory often develop in isolation. This dissertation attempts an explicit bridge.}

\subsection{The Challenge Landscape}

\subsection{Design Principles}

\tableminimal
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{3.5cm}p{8.5cm}@{}}
\toprule
\minimalhead{Design Principle} & \minimalhead{Rationale and Application} \\
\midrule
\textbf{DP1: Theory-first data selection} & Let theoretical questions drive data collection, not data availability. For each modality, document what theoretical constructs it operationalizes. \\
\addlinespace
\textbf{DP2: Modular pipeline architecture} & Build independent processing pipelines for each data source. Enables gradual expansion, parallel development, and selective deployment. \\
\addlinespace
\textbf{DP3: Explicit entity resolution} & Treat identity alignment as a first-class problem. Same organizations appear differently across sources; invest in robust entity resolution early. \\
\addlinespace
\textbf{DP4: Divergence as signal} & Design integration to detect and preserve discrepancies between sources. Divergence reveals tensions, not just measurement error. \\
\addlinespace
\textbf{DP5: Human-in-the-loop validation} & Computational extraction requires human judgment for ambiguous cases. Build calibration, feedback, and correction mechanisms into workflows. \\
\addlinespace
\textbf{DP6: Layered abstraction} & Provide multiple access points: raw data for advanced users, processed features for analysts, visualizations for practitioners. \\
\addlinespace
\textbf{DP7: Reproducible documentation} & Document not just code but decisions, assumptions, and limitations. Future users inherit not just tools but understanding. \\

\labtab{design-principles}

\subsection{Design Patterns}

Beyond principles, we identify recurring \textit{patterns}---reusable solutions to common design challenges:\sidenote{\textbf{Design patterns}---Proven solutions to recurring problems that can be adapted to new contexts. Patterns encode tacit design expertise in transmissible form.}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0.5cm, itemsep=0.3em]
\item[Multi-view triangulation pattern] Combine claimed (self-presentation), attributed (external perception), and enacted (behavioral) role signals. Apply divergence thresholds to flag actors requiring qualitative investigation.

\item[Temporal snapshot pipeline] For evolving phenomena, build pipelines that can be re-run at intervals. Store timestamped snapshots; compute deltas between periods.

\item[Semantic embedding integration] Use embedding spaces (word2vec, BERT) to bridge vocabularies across sources. Map diverse terminology to shared latent dimensions.

\item[Network-text co-analysis] Combine network structure (who connects to whom) with textual content (what they say). Clusters should be coherent in both topology and semantics.

\item[Graduated automation] Start with fully manual annotation to establish ground truth; incrementally automate while preserving human checkpoints for edge cases.

\item[LLM-based knowledge graph extraction] For any text corpus (news articles, Wikipedia summaries, organizational descriptions), apply knowledge graph extraction using tools like \texttt{kg-gen} with local LLMs (Llama 3.2, Mistral). This produces structured entity-relation-entity triples that can be visualized as networks, enabling rapid exploration of actor relationships and conceptual structures within each data modality. The interactive visualizations in each \texttt{rolebox-*} module's \texttt{ui/} folder demonstrate this pattern.
\end{description}

\subsection{Practitioner Guidelines}

For those who would replicate or adapt this approach, we distill practical guidelines:\sidenote{\textbf{Practitioner transfer}---Design knowledge becomes actionable when translated to concrete steps others can follow.}

\begin{importantbox}
\textbf{Starting a Multimodal Cartography Project: Key Steps}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.2em]
\item \textbf{Define scope and actors.} Who are you mapping? What boundaries define inclusion?
\item \textbf{Audit data sources.} For each modality: What exists? Who has access? What coverage gaps?
\item \textbf{Map constructs to modalities.} Which theoretical constructs will each source operationalize?
\item \textbf{Build entity resolution first.} Before any analysis, align identities across sources.
\item \textbf{Start with one modality.} Develop full pipeline for one source before expanding.
\item \textbf{Triangulate early.} Even partial multi-source integration reveals value.
\item \textbf{Document continuously.} Decisions made now become puzzles later if undocumented.
\end{enumerate}
\end{importantbox}

\begin{insightbox}[The Design Knowledge Contribution]
The artifacts developed in this dissertation (RoleBox, RoleSim, modality pipelines) are \emph{instantiations}---specific solutions to the EIT mapping problem. The design knowledge (principles, patterns, guidelines) is the \emph{transferable residue}---what others can apply when building their own multimodal research platforms for different domains.
\end{insightbox}

\section{Practical Implications}[Implications]
\labsec{practical-implications}
%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

What do these findings mean for practitioners---policymakers, intermediary organizations, and transition managers?

\subsection{For Transition Governance}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0.5cm, itemsep=0.3em]
\item[Map before intervening.] Governance interventions should be informed by understanding existing role constellations.\sidenote{\textbf{Diagnostic cartography}---Before prescribing solutions, understand the existing landscape. Many policy failures stem from inadequate situational awareness.} What functions are covered? Where are gaps? What dynamics are underway?

\item[Design for emergence.] Top-down role assignment rarely works. Instead, create conditions (resources, connections, legitimacy) that enable desired roles to emerge.

\item[Monitor constellation health.] Track not just individual actors but constellation-level properties: functional coverage, structural resilience, adaptive capacity.

\item[Expect non-linearity.] Policy interventions may have delayed, amplified, or counterintuitive effects. Build in feedback mechanisms and adaptive capacity.
\end{description}

\subsection{For Intermediary Organizations}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0.5cm, itemsep=0.3em]
\item[Know your position.] Use multimodal analysis to understand how you are perceived across channels.\sidenote{\textbf{Self-awareness}---Organizations often have blind spots about how they are perceived externally. Cross-modal analysis reveals these gaps.} Where do claimed and attributed roles diverge?

\item[Manage gaps strategically.] Some divergence is healthy---aspirational positioning creates room for growth. But persistent gaps between claims and enactment erode legitimacy.

\item[Differentiate or connect.] The two viable strategies in crowded ecosystems: specialize (create unique value) or bridge (connect others). Trying to do everything risks doing nothing distinctively.

\item[Build for resilience.] Role positions are not permanent. Develop adaptive capacity to shift as landscapes change.
\end{description}

\subsection{For Innovation Ecosystems}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0.5cm, itemsep=0.3em]
\item[Functional coverage matters.] Ecosystems need diverse roles---not just entrepreneurs and investors, but also connectors, legitimators, capacity builders, and critics.\sidenote{\textbf{Role diversity}---Healthy ecosystems require the full complement of functions. Over-emphasis on any single role type creates fragility.}

\item[Redundancy provides resilience.] Having multiple actors capable of filling critical roles buffers against disruption.

\item[Boundaries are permeable.] Ecosystems are not closed systems. Actors participate in multiple constellations; resources and ideas flow across boundaries.
\end{description}

\section{Limitations}
\labsec{limitations}
%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

This research has several important limitations that bound the generalizability of findings:\sidenote{\textbf{Honest boundaries}---Acknowledging limitations is not weakness but intellectual honesty. Others can address what we could not.}

\subsection{Empirical Scope}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.3em]
\item \textbf{Single ecosystem.} The EIT is one institutional configuration among many. Findings may not transfer to other innovation ecosystem models (clusters, incubators, living labs).

\item \textbf{European context.} EIT operates within European innovation policy. Role dynamics in other regions (US venture ecosystems, Chinese state-led innovation) likely differ.

\item \textbf{Time period.} Data collection occurred 2020--2023. Role constellations evolve; these are snapshots, not eternal truths.

\item \textbf{Sectoral variation.} Eight intermediation domains provide variation, but conclusions about ``sustainability transitions'' writ large require caution.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Methodological Constraints}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.3em]
\item \textbf{Digital traces only.} Multimodal cartography captures what leaves digital footprints \cite{ruths2014social,boyd2012critical}. Offline interactions, tacit knowledge, informal networks remain invisible.

\item \textbf{English language bias.} Text analysis focused on English content. Non-English actors and communications are underrepresented.

\item \textbf{Computational limitations.} NLP techniques introduce noise. Role extraction is imperfect. Results should be triangulated with qualitative validation.

\item \textbf{Data access barriers.} Some sources (Crunchbase, LinkedIn) have access restrictions that limit reproducibility.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Theoretical Boundaries}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.3em]
\item \textbf{Micro-meso focus.} The dissertation examines actor-level and constellation-level dynamics but does not fully address macro-level regime change.

\item \textbf{Descriptive orientation.} The analysis is primarily descriptive and analytical. Normative questions (what \textit{should} constellations look like?) remain underexplored.

\item \textbf{CAS assumptions.} The complex adaptive systems framing carries assumptions about system boundaries, agent rationality, and adaptation mechanisms that may not hold in all contexts.
\end{itemize}
