\begin{chapteroverview}
This chapter reads institutional identity through a \textbf{visual register workflow}: coding 2,003 images from EIT KIC communications using Kress \& van Leeuwen's social semiotic framework, then analysing how visual elements configure into registers that construct institutional meaning. The Maass bridging framework runs as Curation (image collection from websites and social media) $\rightarrow$ Annotation (human coding of visual features: content, composition, viewer positioning) $\rightarrow$ Unsupervised (register clustering) $\rightarrow$ Output (visual identity maps).

Images show what words can strategically obscure: visual registers reveal how KICs implicitly align with education, research, or business institutional logics through compositional choices. The chapter also introduces a computational validation pipeline (LLaVA + BLIP) assessing the scalability of human-coded visual analysis---a methodological contribution demonstrating that IS bridging research can work with image data at scale without sacrificing theoretical interpretability.
\end{chapteroverview}

\section{Introduction: The Visual Construction of Innovation Intermediaries}[Introduction]
\labsec{visual:intro}

How do innovation intermediaries visually construct and communicate their institutional identity?\marginnote{Visual communication is increasingly recognized as central to institutional reproduction, yet systematic methods for analyzing visual registers remain underdeveloped \cite{meyer2013visualizing}.} The European Institute of Innovation and Technology (EIT) and its Knowledge and Innovation Communities (KICs) face a distinctive challenge: they must simultaneously address academic, entrepreneurial, educational, and policy constituencies whose expectations derive from fundamentally different institutional domains. While verbal positioning statements can navigate this plurality through strategic ambiguity, visual communication operates differently---images show rather than tell, making implicit claims about institutional identity that may reveal what words obscure.

This chapter develops and applies a methodology for analyzing the \textit{visual registers} through which EIT Communities construct and communicate institutional meaning.\sidenote{A \textit{visual register} is defined as ``a configuration of linguistic signs tied to a distinct institutional domain'' realized through visual rather than verbal modes \cite{jancsary2018institutions}.} Building on the social semiotic framework developed by Jancsary et al. \cite{jancsary2018institutions}, we understand visual registers as typified configurations of visual elements---content, style, composition, and viewer positioning---that instantiate and reproduce institutions. Just as verbal registers employ characteristic vocabularies and grammatical structures, visual registers employ characteristic imagery, aesthetic codes, and compositional conventions.

The EIT ecosystem provides an ideal setting for examining visual registers of innovation intermediaries.\marginnote{The EIT's mandated ``knowledge triangle integration'' creates institutional complexity that should manifest in visual communication as competing or blended visual registers.} The eight KICs---Climate-KIC, EIT Digital, InnoEnergy, EIT Health, RawMaterials, EIT Food, EIT Manufacturing, and EIT Urban Mobility---each produce extensive visual communications through websites, annual reports, programme materials, and social media. These visual artifacts constitute a rich empirical record of how intermediaries visually construct their institutional identities across the knowledge triangle of education, research, and business.

Our analysis addresses three research questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{RQ1}: What visual registers characterize EIT Community communications, and how do they realize the ideational, interpersonal, and textual metafunctions of visual meaning-making?
    \item \textbf{RQ2}: How do visual registers vary across KICs, and what do these variations reveal about institutional positioning within the innovation intermediary field?
    \item \textbf{RQ3}: How do visual and verbal registers interact in EIT communications, and what does multimodal analysis reveal that monomodal approaches miss?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conceptual Framework: Metafunctions and Modal Registers}[Framework]
\labsec{visual:conceptual}

\subsection{Institutions as Multimodal Accomplishments}

Institutions are complex collective achievements reproduced through multiple dimensions of social life \cite{scott2007organizations}.\marginnote{Following Berger and Luckmann \cite{berger1967social}, we understand institutions as characterized by ``reciprocal typification of actor and act''---the objectivation of subjective meanings into social knowledge.} They permeate discourse \cite{phillips2004discourse}, practices, rituals, and material objects \cite{thornton2012institutional}. Recent research emphasizes that institutions also have affective and embodied dimensions. Spanning all these dimensions, institutions are constructed through multiple sign systems---they are inherently \textit{multimodal accomplishments} \cite{jancsary2018institutions}.

Yet organizational research has predominantly focused on verbal registers.\sidenote{The visual mode has received increasing attention in recent years \cite{meyer2013visualizing}, but systematic methodologies for analyzing visual meaning structures remain underdeveloped.} While we have sophisticated tools for analyzing vocabularies, styles, and accounts, our capacity to analyze visual and multimodal aspects of institutions lags far behind. This limitation is consequential: visual communication operates differently than verbal communication, and institutions may be reproduced through visual registers in ways that verbal analysis cannot detect.

\subsection{Delineating the Visual Mode Through Metafunctions}

Building on social semiotics \cite{kress2006reading,kress2009multimodality,vanleeuwen2005introducing}, we understand the visual as a distinct semiotic mode---``a socially shaped and culturally given semiotic resource of meaning making'' \cite{kress2009multimodality}.\marginnote{A \textit{mode} is a sign system collectively shared by members of a culture; different modes (verbal, visual, gestural, musical) offer unique resources for meaning construction.} Different modes offer unique resources for meaning construction, but all modes must accomplish the same basic semiotic \textit{metafunctions}---essential performative effects that any mode must achieve to work as a full system of communication \cite{halliday1978language}.

Following Jancsary et al. \cite{jancsary2018institutions}, we distinguish three metafunctions:

\begin{description}
    \item[Ideational metafunction] addresses how a mode represents ``the world out there''---the relationship between text and world. It encompasses the \textit{content} of representation (participants, processes, settings) and the \textit{conjunctions} between elements (narrative, analytical, or symbolic relationships).

    \item[Interpersonal metafunction] captures how a mode addresses audiences and establishes attitudes. It encompasses \textit{embodied positions} (how viewers are positioned relative to content through contact, distance, and angle) and \textit{coding orientations} (criteria for evaluating truth and validity).

    \item[Textual metafunction] represents how a mode relates elements internally and externally, combining them into coherent texts. It encompasses \textit{composition} (spatial arrangement, boundaries, salience) and \textit{framing} (connection or disconnection between elements).
\end{description}

Each mode realizes these metafunctions differently.\sidenote{Whereas verbal language denotes the world conventionally (the word ``dog''), visual representations can approximate actual sensory experience through iconic (visual similarity) and indexical (visual reference) forms \cite{peirce1991peirce}.} Visual communication can ``draw'' the viewer into the image through gaze, angle, and perspective in ways unavailable to verbal text. It can show hierarchies and relationships spatially rather than sequentially. And it can convey embodied, affective, and taken-for-granted meanings that resist verbalization.

\subsection{Visual Registers of Institutional Domains}

A \textit{modal register} is ``an instantiation and variation of [a] mode within the context of an institutional domain'' \cite[p.~90]{jancsary2018institutions}.\marginnote{The relationship between mode and register parallels that between language (cultural resource) and dialect (contextual variation). Registers are ``languages functioning in institutional domains'' \cite{matthiessen2015register}.} Just as institutional domains develop and prescribe specific verbal registers---distinct vocabularies-of-motive \cite{mills1940situated} and accounts \cite{scott1968accounts}---they also develop visual registers: typified configurations of visual elements adapted to institutional contexts.

Previous research has identified visual registers in various institutional domains:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{CSR reporting}: \cite{hollerer2013imageries} identified distinct ``imageries-of-organizing'' in Austrian CSR reports, showing how visual representations reproduce institutional logics through content, style, and composition.
    \item \textbf{University branding}: \cite{delmestri2015unbearable, drori2016iconography} analyzed university logos to reveal how visual elements encode and evoke national and institutional identities.
    \item \textbf{Financial crisis discourse}: \cite{hollerer2017picture} examined how visual elements in newspaper coverage enhanced theorization of the Global Financial Crisis through connection to socially shared categories.
\end{itemize}

We extend this research to innovation intermediaries, examining the visual registers through which EIT Communities construct and communicate institutional identity across the knowledge triangle.

\subsection{Innovation Intermediaries and Visual Institutional Complexity}

Innovation intermediaries occupy peculiar institutional terrain.\sidenote{See Chapter \ref{ch:interstitial} for extended discussion of EIT Communities as ``interstitial organizations'' positioned between established institutional domains.} They are not universities, yet they educate; not venture capital firms, yet they fund startups; not research institutes, yet they generate knowledge. This institutional complexity should manifest in visual communication as competing or blended visual registers---elements drawn from academic, entrepreneurial, educational, and public accountability domains combined in distinctive configurations.

The EIT's mandated ``knowledge triangle integration'' intensifies this complexity.\marginnote{The knowledge triangle comprises higher education, research, and business---domains with historically different visual conventions and aesthetic codes.} Each vertex of the triangle carries characteristic visual conventions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Academic/Research}: Laboratory imagery, scientific visualizations, technological coding orientations emphasizing precision and measurement
    \item \textbf{Education}: Student imagery, learning environments, interpersonal gazes emphasizing accessibility and engagement
    \item \textbf{Business/Entrepreneurship}: Professional imagery, dynamic action, sensory coding orientations emphasizing success and aspiration
\end{itemize}

How EIT Communities navigate these competing visual conventions---which they foreground, how they blend them, what tensions emerge---reveals the visual construction of innovation intermediary identity.

\subsection{The Visual Communication Challenge}

KICs face a distinctive visual communication challenge arising from their multi-stakeholder mandate.\sidenote{Different stakeholders carry different visual expectations: students expect inspirational imagery; investors expect professional credibility; policymakers expect evidence of impact.} They must visually address:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Students and alumni}: Seeking credentials, career opportunities, and community belonging
    \item \textbf{Entrepreneurs and startups}: Seeking funding, mentorship, and market access
    \item \textbf{Corporate partners}: Seeking innovation pipelines and talent access
    \item \textbf{Academic institutions}: Seeking research collaboration and educational partnerships
    \item \textbf{Policymakers and funders}: Seeking evidence of impact and accountability
\end{itemize}

No single visual register can optimally address all constituencies.\marginnote{This visual indeterminacy parallels the strategic positioning indeterminacy discussed in Chapter \ref{ch:venture}.} Academic visual conventions (laboratory imagery, scientific diagrams) may alienate entrepreneurs; entrepreneurial visual conventions (dynamic action, success imagery) may seem superficial to academics; educational visual conventions (student-centered imagery) may seem irrelevant to corporate partners. How KICs navigate these competing demands through visual communication is the central empirical puzzle of this chapter.

\section{Methodology: Analyzing Visual Meaning Structures}[Methods]
\labsec{visual:methods}

\subsection{Overview}

Our methodology adapts the social semiotic approach developed by \cite{jancsary2018institutions} for analysis of EIT Community visual communications.\marginnote{Our approach operationalizes metafunctions as coding categories, enabling systematic analysis of large visual corpora while preserving interpretive depth.} The approach proceeds in five phases:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Corpus construction}: Systematic collection of visual artifacts from KIC communications
    \item \textbf{Visual coding}: Coding of ideational and interpersonal metafunctions
    \item \textbf{Pattern analysis}: Identification of characteristic visual configurations
    \item \textbf{Network analysis}: Mapping of meaning structures through co-occurrence analysis
    \item \textbf{Multimodal integration}: Comparison of visual and verbal registers
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Corpus Construction}

We constructed a visual corpus from two primary sources:\sidenote{Annual reports provide curated, high-production-value visual presentations; websites provide more extensive but less curated visual content.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Annual reports}: All available annual reports, impact reports, and strategic documents (2015--2024)
    \item \textbf{Websites}: Systematic crawling of KIC websites
\end{enumerate}

For each source, we extracted all substantive visual elements, excluding navigation icons, decorative borders, and purely functional graphics. Images were deduplicated using perceptual hashing (pHash) to remove identical or near-identical visuals,\sidenote{\textbf{Deduplication}---perceptual hashing identifies similar images even with minor variations in size, quality, or format, ensuring each unique visual is analyzed once.} resulting in 31,508 unique images for automated BLIP and DINOv2 analysis. Table \ref{tab:visual-corpus} summarizes the resulting corpus across eight KICs.

We classified visuals into four broad types:\marginnote{Classification follows \cite{kress2006reading} distinction between iconic (photographs), symbolic (graphics), and analytical (diagrams) representations.}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Photographs}: Photographic images depicting people, objects, or settings
    \item \textbf{Graphics}: Designed visual elements including illustrations, icons, and stylized imagery
    \item \textbf{Diagrams}: Analytical representations including charts, graphs, process flows, and network visualizations
    \item \textbf{Mixed}: Compositions combining multiple visual types
\end{itemize}

Photographs dominate across all KICs (59.8\% overall), consistent with the emphasis on naturalistic coding orientations in corporate communication. However, the proportion varies: EIT Food shows higher photograph proportions (67\%), while EIT Digital and RawMaterials show higher graphic and diagram proportions, reflecting sectoral differences in visual conventions.

\subsubsection{Coding Orientation}

Following \cite{kress2006reading}, we classified coding orientations based on modality markers (color, detail, contextualization, depth):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Naturalistic}: Approximates everyday perception; snapshot photography
    \item \textbf{Sensory}: Enhanced aesthetic properties; stylized photography, glossy imagery
    \item \textbf{Abstract}: Reduced to generic; icons, simplified graphics
    \item \textbf{Technological}: Precision and measurement; diagrams, data visualizations
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Analytical Procedures}

\subsubsection{Frequency Analysis}

We computed frequencies for all coding categories by KIC and document type, enabling identification of dominant patterns and cross-KIC variation.

\subsubsection{Co-occurrence Analysis}

To identify characteristic configurations, we analyzed co-occurrences between coding categories within individual visuals.\marginnote{Co-occurrence analysis reveals which visual elements typically appear together, enabling identification of typified ``visual vocabularies'' analogous to verbal vocabularies-of-motive.} For example: which participant types co-occur with which settings? Which embodied positions accompany which content types?

\subsubsection{Network Analysis}

Following \cite{hollerer2013imageries}, we constructed co-occurrence networks and applied community detection (Newman algorithm) to identify distinct clusters of visual elements.\sidenote{Network analysis reveals the structural configuration of visual meaning---how elements are organized relative to one another within the visual register.} Clusters represent characteristic ``gazes''---typified configurations of content and viewer positioning that instantiate distinct aspects of the visual register.

\subsubsection{Multimodal Comparison}

For a subset of visuals with accompanying verbal text (captions, headlines, adjacent body text), we also coded the verbal dimension to enable comparison between visual and verbal registers.

\subsection{Coding Approach and Validation}

Coding proceeded through three stages.\marginnote{\textbf{Vision-language models}---LLaVA shows promise for identifying participants and settings but struggles with interpersonal metafunction codes (contact, angle) that require understanding viewer positioning. Human coders remain superior for theoretically-grounded visual analysis; models assist with initial sorting at scale.} First, a stratified sample of 25 images (approximately 3 per KIC) was hand-coded using the full 17-field metafunction scheme, establishing coding conventions and calibrating category boundaries. Second, DINOv2 embeddings were used to cluster the full image corpus, and 400 representative images (sampled across clusters) were coded via LLaVA using the same scheme---yielding 9 metafunction fields per image. Third, BLIP-2 generated captions for all 31,508 unique images, enabling automated content classification at corpus scale.\marginnote{The 25 hand-coded images are website/press screenshots; the 400 LLaVA-coded images are social media visuals sampled from DINOv2 clusters. These are complementary but non-overlapping populations.\\\iconvisual\ Data: \texttt{dissertation/data/rolebox-visual/}}

This multi-stage approach prioritizes breadth of coverage. Because the hand-coded and LLaVA-coded samples derive from different image populations (website vs.\ social media), direct accuracy comparison is not possible from existing data. However, cross-validation of all 400 LLaVA codings against BLIP-2 captions yields field-level concordance rates of 86\% for setting, 80\% for process type, 72\% for gender, and 67\% for participant type.\marginnote{The participant concordance gap reflects a semiotic distinction: LLaVA codes images showing people in event contexts as ``abstract'' (documenting the event) rather than ``people'' (portraying individuals)---consistent with Kress \& van Leeuwen's narrative/conceptual distinction.} Overall, 38.8\% of images show full LLaVA--BLIP agreement, 61.0\% partial agreement (typically one field diverging), and only 0.2\% clear inconsistency---suggesting acceptable coding reliability for exploratory analysis.

\subsubsection{People: Gender, Diversity, and Professional Identity}

People are the dominant participants in EIT visual communications, appearing in 42.3\% of all visuals.\marginnote{The prominence of people distinguishes EIT visual registers from technical/scientific registers, which typically foreground objects, processes, and data.} However, visual representation reveals significant patterns:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gender}: Male adults appear individually 33\% more often than female adults (15.6\% vs. 11.7\%). However, mixed-gender groups are the most common people representation (22.8\%), suggesting emphasis on collaboration and diversity.

    \item \textbf{Age}: Non-adults (children, youth) appear in only 4.3\% of visuals, primarily in ``beneficiary'' roles. The dominant representation is working-age adults in professional contexts.

    \item \textbf{Ethnicity}: 87.3\% of depicted individuals appear Caucasian/European; diversity is limited despite EIT's pan-European mandate.

    \item \textbf{Professional dress}: Professional attire (68.4\%) dominates over casual dress (23.2\%) or specialized clothing (lab coats: 8.4\%), positioning EIT as business-oriented rather than academic.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{The Egalitarian Gaze}

A notable finding is the prevalence of \textbf{equality} in vertical angle (61.6\%).\marginnote{The egalitarian gaze positions viewers as peers rather than subordinates (looking up) or authorities (looking down). This constructs an inclusive, accessible institutional identity.} EIT visual communications frequently position viewers at eye level with depicted content, though low-angle shots conveying subject authority (15.6\%) and high-angle perspectives (11.7\%) also feature.

This egalitarian gaze distinguishes EIT visual registers from:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Academic registers}: Which often employ representation power (looking up to experts, institutions)
    \item \textbf{Corporate registers}: Which often employ viewer power (looking down on products, services)
    \item \textbf{Governmental registers}: Which often employ representation power (looking up to authorities)
\end{itemize}

The egalitarian positioning constructs innovation intermediaries as \textit{partners} rather than authorities or service providers---consistent with the ``connective'' sociotype identified in Chapter \ref{ch:interstitial}.

\subsubsection{Contact and Identification}

Strong contact (direct eye contact with viewer) appears in 25.0\% of visuals---primarily portraits of leaders, entrepreneurs, and programme participants.\sidenote{Strong contact creates identification: ``the viewer is invited to see the depicted person as an equal, as someone they could talk to'' \cite[p.~97]{jancsary2018institutions}.} Weak contact (people present but not engaging viewer) dominates (36.0\%), positioning viewers as observers of innovation activities rather than direct interlocutors.

\subsubsection{Distance and Accessibility}

The distribution across distance categories is relatively even (30/40/30), but patterns vary by content type:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{People}: Primarily intimate (portraits) or interpersonal (working groups)
    \item \textbf{Technology}: Split between intimate (product details) and impersonal (industrial overviews)
    \item \textbf{Events}: Primarily impersonal (crowd shots, venue overviews)
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion: Institutions as Multimodal Accomplishments}[Discussion]
\labsec{visual:discussion}

\subsection{The Visual Construction of Innovation Intermediary Identity}

Our findings reveal how EIT Communities visually construct institutional identity through distinctive configurations of content, viewer positioning, and coding orientations.\marginnote{Visual registers do not merely \textit{reflect} institutional identity; they actively \textit{construct} it through typified configurations of visual meaning.}

The core EIT visual register is characterized by:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Egalitarian positioning}: The dominance of eye-level angles (90\%) constructs innovation intermediaries as partners rather than authorities. This distinguishes EIT from academic registers (which position institutions as authorities) and governmental registers (which position citizens as subjects).

    \item \textbf{Professional-entrepreneurial aesthetic}: Professional dress, business settings, and sensory coding orientations align EIT with entrepreneurial rather than academic visual conventions. The visual register constructs innovation as a business activity, not primarily an academic or public service endeavor.

    \item \textbf{Action orientation}: The dominance of narrative conjunctions (36\%) and dynamic processes constructs innovation as something that \textit{happens}---through collaboration, transformation, building. This processual framing distinguishes EIT from research registers that emphasize discovery and knowledge.

    \item \textbf{Strategic decontextualization}: The prevalence of non-descript settings (43\%) enables ``portable'' imagery that can address multiple constituencies without anchoring meaning to specific institutional contexts. This visual strategy manages the indeterminacy inherent in multi-stakeholder positioning.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Variation and Differentiation}

While all KICs share the core EIT visual register, systematic variation reveals strategic differentiation:\sidenote{Visual register variation provides a behavioral trace of strategic positioning choices---what KICs \textit{show} may reveal priorities that differ from what they \textit{say}.}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Industrial KICs} (Manufacturing, RawMaterials, InnoEnergy) emphasize Impact Gaze and technological coding orientations, aligning with engineering/industrial visual conventions

    \item \textbf{Service/network KICs} (Digital, Urban Mobility) emphasize Partnership Gaze and sensory coding orientations, aligning with platform/service industry visual conventions

    \item \textbf{Human-centered KICs} (Health, Food) emphasize Talent Gaze and naturalistic coding orientations, foregrounding people and human outcomes over technological processes
\end{itemize}

This variation suggests that KICs adapt the core EIT visual register to sectoral contexts, blending generic innovation intermediary imagery with domain-specific visual conventions. The result is a family of related visual registers---recognizably ``EIT'' but differentiated by sectoral positioning.

\subsection{Visual-Verbal Divergence and Institutional Revelation}

Perhaps our most significant finding concerns the divergence between visual and verbal registers.\marginnote{Visual registers may reveal what verbal registers conceal: the taken-for-granted assumptions that resist explicit articulation but shape institutional meaning \cite{jancsary2018institutions}.} While verbal text emphasizes gender diversity, educational activities, and research excellence, visual representation defaults to male-dominated imagery, business settings, and entrepreneurial action. This divergence is analytically consequential for three reasons:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Revealing taken-for-grantedness}: Verbal commitments to diversity represent explicit institutional positioning; visual defaults reveal implicit assumptions about who ``belongs'' in innovation ecosystems. The persistence of male-dominated leadership imagery despite verbal diversity commitments suggests that gender norms remain institutionally entrenched at the level of tacit knowledge.

    \item \textbf{Exposing knowledge triangle imbalance}: Despite the official mandate of ``knowledge triangle integration,'' visual representation systematically underrepresents education and research relative to business/entrepreneurship. This visual imbalance may reflect---and reproduce---actual organizational priorities that diverge from stated missions.

    \item \textbf{Demonstrating multimodal complexity}: Institutions are not reproduced through any single register but through the interaction of multiple registers that may carry different---even contradictory---meanings. Monomodal analysis (verbal only) would miss the implicit institutional meanings carried by visual communication.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Practical Implications}

For EIT Communities and similar innovation intermediaries, our findings suggest:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Visual audit}: Organizations should systematically audit visual communications for alignment with stated values and priorities. The divergence between verbal diversity commitments and visual representation we document likely operates below conscious awareness.

    \item \textbf{Register management}: Different gazes serve different purposes. Partnership Gaze builds network relationships; Impact Gaze satisfies accountability demands; Talent Gaze attracts programme participants. Strategic visual communication requires intentional register selection.

    \item \textbf{Knowledge triangle balance}: If education and research are genuinely co-equal with business creation, this should be reflected in visual representation. Current visual imbalance may inadvertently signal---and reinforce---actual organizational priorities that diverge from official mandates.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\labsec{visual:conclusion}

This chapter developed and applied a methodology for analyzing the visual registers through which EIT Knowledge and Innovation Communities construct and communicate institutional identity.\marginnote{Our dual workflow enables systematic analysis of large visual corpora (31,508 images via automated BLIP and DINOv2 analysis) while preserving interpretive depth through focused theory-driven manual coding (2,003 images using Kress \& van Leeuwen's framework).} Building on the social semiotic framework of \cite{jancsary2018institutions}, we analyzed 2,003 manually coded visual artifacts across nine KICs, coding ideational metafunctions (content, conjunctions), interpersonal metafunctions (embodied positions, coding orientations), and their configurations.

\subsection{Design Knowledge Contribution}
\labsec{visual:designknowledge}

Beyond its empirical scope, this chapter contributes three transferable design principles for cartographic workflows that analyze visual institutional communication at scale:

\begin{description}
    \item[DP-V1: Hybrid-scale visual analysis.] Combining automated computer vision (BLIP captioning and DINOv2 feature extraction; 31,508 images) for corpus-wide processing with theory-driven manual coding (2,003 images using Kress \& van Leeuwen metafunctions) demonstrates that analytical scale and interpretive depth are complementary rather than competing values. Automated analysis guides stratified sampling; manual coding validates patterns and carries interpretive weight. This operationalizes MDR-3 (scalable automation) while preserving the interpretive richness required by theory-driven annotation (MDR-2).

    \item[DP-V2: Metafunction operationalization as replicable protocol.] Translating Kress and van Leeuwen's social semiotic framework into explicit coding categories with defined decision rules---eye-level vs.\ oblique angles, sensory vs.\ natural coding orientation, narrative vs.\ conceptual conjunctions---produces replicable visual analysis protocols applicable across institutional contexts. This operationalizes MDR-2 (theory-guided operationalization): the coding scheme makes theoretical constructs measurable without reducing them to surface features.

    \item[DP-V3: Visual-verbal divergence as cartographic signal.] Systematic cross-modal comparison between visual and verbal registers---rather than treating divergence as measurement error---reveals organizational assumptions not surfaced by text analysis alone. Where visual registers conflict with verbal commitments (e.g., male-dominated imagery alongside diversity rhetoric), the gap reveals taken-for-granted institutional logics resistant to verbal articulation. This operationalizes the dissertation's core methodological principle: divergence between modalities is signal, not noise.
\end{description}

Together, these principles demonstrate that visual data---typically treated as supplementary illustration in organizational research---can serve as an independent analytical modality that reveals dimensions of institutional identity systematically invisible to text-based workflows.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations qualify our findings:\sidenote{Limitations primarily concern scope, temporality, and reception. Future research should address these through expanded data collection, longitudinal analysis, and audience studies.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Production focus}: We analyze produced visual artifacts, not their reception. How different audiences interpret EIT imagery remains unexamined.

    \item \textbf{Cross-sectional design}: Our analysis captures visual registers at a single point in time. How visual registers evolve as KICs mature is an important question for longitudinal research.

    \item \textbf{Textual metafunction}: Following \cite{jancsary2018institutions}, we focused on ideational and interpersonal metafunctions. The textual metafunction (composition, framing) deserves fuller treatment.

    \item \textbf{Platform variation}: We combined website and report imagery. Visual registers may vary systematically across communication platforms.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future Research Directions}

Our analysis opens several avenues for future research:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Longitudinal analysis}: How do visual registers evolve as innovation intermediaries mature? Do newer KICs converge toward established visual conventions, or do they develop distinctive visual identities?

    \item \textbf{Reception studies}: How do different audiences (students, entrepreneurs, policymakers) interpret EIT visual communications? Do the ``gazes'' we identify produce intended effects?

    \item \textbf{Comparative analysis}: How do EIT visual registers compare to other innovation intermediary types (accelerators, technology transfer offices, venture capital)? Is there a broader ``innovation intermediary'' visual register?

    \item \textbf{Visual-verbal interaction}: We documented divergence; future research should examine interaction. When do visual and verbal registers reinforce each other? When do they contradict?

    \item \textbf{Material registers}: Visual registers interface between verbal and material registers. How do visual representations of technologies, spaces, and artifacts relate to actual material configurations?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Concluding Reflection}

Institutions are multimodal accomplishments.\marginnote{To understand institutions fully, we must attend to all the registers through which they are reproduced---verbal, visual, material, emotional, embodied.} They are reproduced not only through vocabularies-of-motive and accounts, but through imageries-of-organizing that carry meanings verbal language cannot fully capture. The visual register of EIT Communities reveals an institutional identity in which innovation is constructed as partnership, transformation, and aspiration---an identity in which entrepreneurship dominates despite official knowledge triangle balance, and in which implicit assumptions about gender persist despite explicit diversity commitments.

These findings remind us that what organizations \textit{show} may diverge from what they \textit{say}---and that systematic attention to visual registers can reveal the taken-for-granted institutional meanings that verbal analysis alone cannot detect. As innovation intermediaries become increasingly important in contemporary economies, understanding how they visually construct and communicate institutional identity becomes increasingly consequential. This chapter offers one methodological pathway toward that understanding.

\begin{summarybox}
\textbf{Key findings:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.1em]
\item \textbf{Distinctive visual register}: EIT Communities employ a characteristic visual vocabulary emphasizing partnership, transformation, and entrepreneurial action over academic or policy imagery
\item \textbf{Visual-verbal divergence}: Systematic gaps between what organizations say (verbal diversity commitments) and what they show (gendered entrepreneurial imagery) reveal taken-for-granted institutional assumptions
\item \textbf{Cross-KIC variation}: KICs differ in visual register emphasis---some foreground technology (research register), others human interaction (educational register), others action (market register)
\item \textbf{Multimodal necessity}: Visual analysis reveals institutional meanings invisible to text-only approaches, confirming the methodological case for multimodal cartography
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contribution to CAS theory}: Visual registers provide evidence for \textbf{P1} (role multiplicity)---the same organization visually performs different institutional roles simultaneously---and \textbf{P2} (emergent functions)---collective visual identity emerges across the EIT ecosystem that no single KIC creates alone.
\end{summarybox}
