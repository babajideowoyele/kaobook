\section{Introduction: Mapping the EIT Venture Landscape}[Introduction]
\labsec{venture:intro}

What does an innovation intermediary's portfolio reveal about its strategic positioning?\marginnote{Portfolio composition---the ventures an intermediary funds, accelerates, or co-invests in---provides a behavioural trace of strategic priorities distinct from stated missions.} The ventures that an organization chooses to fund, accelerate, and support constitute a behavioural signature of strategic intent that complements---and sometimes contradicts---official mission statements. While intermediaries may articulate broad mandates encompassing education, research, and business creation, their portfolio choices reveal which types of ventures they actually prioritize and how they position themselves within the broader innovation landscape.

The European Institute of Innovation and Technology (EIT) and its eight Knowledge and Innovation Communities (KICs) have collectively supported thousands of ventures across Europe since 2010.\sidenote{EIT support includes acceleration programmes, grant funding, equity investment, and access to corporate partners. See \url{https://eit.europa.eu} for current portfolio data.} These ventures---spanning climate technology, digital innovation, healthcare, food systems, energy, raw materials, manufacturing, and urban mobility---constitute a rich empirical record of how each KIC translates its sectoral mandate into concrete investment and support decisions. By analyzing the positioning statements these ventures use to describe themselves, we can reconstruct the strategic landscapes within which EIT Communities operate.

This chapter employs visual analytic methods to examine the structure of venture positioning across EIT Community portfolios.\marginnote{Our approach extends \cite{basole2019visual}, who analyzed 60,000 ventures across 35 global entrepreneurial ecosystems using text-based similarity networks.} Building on the pioneering work of \cite{basole2019visual}, we analyze venture business descriptions from Crunchbase---a comprehensive database of technology companies, investors, and funding events---to map the positioning landscapes of EIT-supported ventures. We extend the Basole et al. methodology in two ways. First, we focus on a deliberately constructed innovation ecosystem (the EIT) rather than geographically emergent ones, enabling examination of how institutional design shapes portfolio composition. Second, we complement traditional TF-IDF approaches with state-of-the-art transformer-based embeddings, comparing how different representation methods reveal distinct structural properties.

Our analysis addresses three research questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{RQ1}: What is the structure of venture positioning within each KIC's portfolio, and how do positioning clusters map onto---or diverge from---official sectoral mandates?
    \item \textbf{RQ2}: How do positioning landscapes differ across KICs, and what do these differences reveal about strategic differentiation among innovation intermediaries?
    \item \textbf{RQ3}: How do traditional (TF-IDF) and modern (transformer embedding) text representation methods compare in revealing positioning structure?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background: Venture Positioning in Innovation Ecosystems}[Background]
\labsec{venture:background}

\subsection{Entrepreneurial Ecosystems and Portfolio Composition}

The concept of entrepreneurial ecosystems has gained substantial traction in both scholarly research and policy discourse \cite{isenberg2010big,stam2015entrepreneurial,adner2017ecosystem}.\marginnote{Entrepreneurial ecosystems comprise interdependent actors---entrepreneurs, investors, universities, support organizations---within bounded contexts \cite{spigel2017relational}.} An entrepreneurial ecosystem can be defined as a set of interdependent organizations engaged in productive entrepreneurship within a geographically or institutionally defined territory \cite{cohen2006sustainable} \cite{acs2017lineages}. The ecosystem metaphor emphasizes that value creation emerges from complex interactions among heterogeneous actors rather than from individual organizational efforts \cite{adner2017ecosystem}.

Research on ecosystem structure has evolved from early work on industrial clusters \cite{porter1998clusters} and regional innovation systems \cite{cooke2001regional} toward more relational perspectives emphasizing network topology and actor positioning \cite{spigel2017relational}.\sidenote{See \cite{acs2017lineages} for a comprehensive review of the intellectual lineages connecting cluster, innovation system, and ecosystem research.} A key insight from this evolution is that ecosystems are characterized not only by the presence of particular actor types but by the relational configurations among them. Two ecosystems with similar compositional profiles may exhibit very different dynamics depending on how actors are connected and positioned relative to one another.

Innovation intermediaries occupy distinctive positions within ecosystems.\marginnote{Innovation intermediaries include accelerators, incubators, technology transfer offices, and organizations like EIT KICs that span multiple functions \cite{howells2006intermediation,kivimaa2019innovation}.} They do not produce innovations directly but rather facilitate connections, provide resources, and orchestrate activities among other ecosystem participants \cite{howells2006intermediation,klerkx2012dealing,vanLente2003roles}. Their portfolios---the ventures they choose to support---reveal strategic priorities and positioning choices that shape the broader ecosystem structure.

\subsection{Strategic Positioning Through Portfolio Composition}

The literature on strategic positioning has predominantly focused on how individual organizations differentiate themselves from competitors while maintaining legitimacy with stakeholders \cite{porter1980competitive,deephouse1999same,porac1989competitive}.\marginnote{The conformity-differentiation tradeoff is central to strategic positioning: organizations must be different enough to capture value but similar enough to be recognized as legitimate \cite{zhao2017optimal}.} Organizations face a fundamental tension between differentiation (standing out) and conformity (fitting in). Those that deviate too far from established templates risk incomprehensibility; those that conform too closely risk competitive indistinction.

For innovation intermediaries, portfolio composition represents a key dimension of strategic positioning.\sidenote{Portfolio analysis has been applied extensively in venture capital research \cite{gompers2009specialization} but less systematically to public innovation intermediaries.} The ventures an intermediary chooses to support signal its strategic priorities to multiple audiences: potential applicants learn what types of ventures are likely to receive support; co-investors learn about sectoral focus and risk appetite; policymakers learn about alignment with public objectives. Portfolio choices thus function as positioning statements enacted through behaviour rather than articulated through language.

Crucially, portfolio positioning may diverge from stated mission.\marginnote{Decoupling between formal mission and actual practice is a well-documented phenomenon in institutional theory \cite{meyer1977institutionalized}.} An intermediary may articulate a broad mandate while concentrating support on a narrow subset of ventures; conversely, an intermediary with a focused mandate may support ventures that stretch or transcend official boundaries. Analyzing the positioning statements of portfolio ventures reveals the \textit{de facto} strategic landscape within which an intermediary operates---a landscape that may differ substantially from \textit{de jure} mission statements.

\subsection{Text-Based Analysis of Venture Positioning}

Basole et al. \cite{basole2019visual} pioneered the application of text-based similarity analysis to entrepreneurial ecosystems.\marginnote{Basole et al. \cite{basole2019visual} analyzed 58,880 ventures across 35 global ecosystems, revealing that positioning clusters do not map cleanly onto industry classifications.} Analyzing nearly 60,000 venture descriptions from Crunchbase, they demonstrated that ventures from different industries often cluster together when they employ similar positioning language. This finding suggests that strategic positioning operates according to logics distinct from---though intersecting with---industrial categorization.

The Basole et al. methodology employs TF-IDF (term frequency-inverse document frequency) weighting to convert textual descriptions into numerical vectors, cosine similarity to compute pairwise venture similarity, and network visualization to reveal cluster structure.\sidenote{TF-IDF weighting captures the intuition that terms appearing frequently in a document but rarely across the corpus are most informative for characterizing that document.} Community detection algorithms identify groups of ventures with similar positioning, and topic modeling provides interpretable labels for emergent clusters.

Recent advances in natural language processing offer complementary approaches.\marginnote{Transformer models like BERT \cite{devlin2019bert} and sentence transformers \cite{reimers2019sentence} learn contextual word representations that capture semantic nuance beyond keyword matching.} Transformer-based language models learn contextual representations that capture semantic relationships invisible to keyword-based methods. Two ventures might use different words to describe similar value propositions; transformer embeddings can detect this semantic similarity even when surface vocabulary differs. We employ both approaches, comparing their relative strengths for revealing positioning structure.

\section{Data: EIT Venture Portfolios from Crunchbase}[Data]
\labsec{venture:data}

\subsection{Data Source and Extraction}

We use Crunchbase as our primary data source for EIT-supported ventures.\marginnote{Crunchbase (\url{crunchbase.com}) is a wiki-style database of technology companies, people, and investors, widely used in entrepreneurship research \cite{basole2019visual, dalle2017using}.} Crunchbase provides comprehensive coverage of technology ventures including company descriptions, founding dates, funding rounds, investor information, industry categories, and employee counts. The database is community-curated with contributions from executives, entrepreneurs, and investors, supplemented by machine learning validation and manual expert review \cite{basole2019visual}.

We identified EIT-supported ventures through multiple channels:\sidenote{Triangulating across multiple identification methods reduces the risk of systematic omissions.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Direct EIT tagging}: Ventures explicitly tagged with EIT KIC affiliations in Crunchbase
    \item \textbf{Investor matching}: Ventures listing EIT KICs or their investment vehicles as investors
    \item \textbf{Accelerator matching}: Ventures listing EIT-affiliated accelerator programmes
    \item \textbf{Cross-reference validation}: Matching against official EIT portfolio databases and annual reports
\end{enumerate}

Data extraction was conducted via the Crunchbase API in November 2024. We extracted all available fields for identified ventures, with particular attention to the \texttt{short\_description} and \texttt{description} fields that contain positioning statements.

\subsection{Validation}

To assess the quality of Crunchbase descriptions, we followed \cite{basole2019visual} in comparing Crunchbase entries against corporate website descriptions for a random sample of 100 ventures.\marginnote{Validation against corporate websites addresses concerns about Crunchbase data quality and currency.\\\iconinvest\ Data: \texttt{dissertation/data/rolebox-crunchbase/}} Using TF-IDF cosine similarity with a threshold of 0.15, we found that 87\% of sampled ventures had descriptions substantially similar to their corporate websites (mean similarity: 0.62). The 13\% with lower similarity primarily reflected outdated Crunchbase entries for ventures that had pivoted or rebranded; we flagged these cases for manual review.

We additionally validated KIC assignments for a stratified sample of
117 ventures (10 per KIC plus all unclassified cases).
Of 78 ventures with investor-based KIC assignments,
47 (60.3\%) were confirmed correct; the remaining
assignments reflected multi-KIC investors or ambiguous affiliations.
Of 39 initially unclassified ventures, 36 (92.3\%) could be resolved
to a specific KIC through description and industry analysis,
reducing unclassified cases from 6.0\% to 0.5\% of the corpus.

\subsection{TF-IDF Approach (Basole Method)}

\subsubsection{Preprocessing}

Following \cite{basole2019visual}, venture descriptions undergo standard text preprocessing:\sidenote{Preprocessing choices can significantly affect downstream results; we follow established conventions to ensure comparability with prior work.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Tokenization using NLTK
    \item Lowercasing
    \item Stop word removal (English stop words plus domain-specific terms: ``company,'' ``startup,'' ``platform,'' ``solution'')
    \item No stemming or lemmatization (to preserve semantic distinctions)
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{TF-IDF Computation}

We compute TF-IDF weights using the standard formulation:

\begin{equation}
    w_{t,d} = \text{tf}_{t,d} \times \log_2 \frac{|D|}{n_t}
\end{equation}

where $\text{tf}_{t,d}$ is the frequency of term $t$ in document $d$, $|D|$ is corpus size, and $n_t$ is the document frequency of term $t$. We use raw term frequency (not normalized) following \cite{basole2019visual}, and apply $L_2$ normalization to the resulting vectors.

\subsubsection{Similarity Computation}

Pairwise venture similarity is computed using cosine similarity:

\begin{equation}
    \text{sim}(\mathbf{w}_p, \mathbf{w}_q) = \frac{\mathbf{w}_p \cdot \mathbf{w}_q}{\|\mathbf{w}_p\| \|\mathbf{w}_q\|}
\end{equation}

For a corpus of $n = 7,341$ ventures, this requires computing $\frac{n(n-1)}{2} \approx 20.6$ million pairwise similarities. We employ sparse matrix operations and parallel processing to manage computational demands.

\subsubsection{Network Construction}

Following \cite{basole2019visual}, we construct a similarity network $G = (V, E)$ where:\marginnote{The 15\% similarity threshold follows \cite{basole2019visual} and balances network density against noise. We conduct sensitivity analysis across thresholds (10\%, 15\%, 20\%).}

\begin{itemize}
    \item $V$ is the set of ventures
    \item $E$ contains edges $(i, j)$ where $\text{sim}(i, j) \geq \tau$
    \item Edge weight $w_{ij} = \text{sim}(i, j)$
\end{itemize}

We apply a similarity threshold $\tau = 0.15$ to eliminate weak connections, following \cite{basole2019visual}. This threshold retains edges representing substantive positioning similarity while reducing network density to enable meaningful cluster detection.

\subsubsection{Community Detection}

We employ the Louvain algorithm \cite{blondel2008fast} for community detection.\sidenote{The Louvain algorithm optimizes modularity through iterative node reassignment; it is efficient for large networks and does not require specifying the number of clusters \textit{a priori}.} Louvain maximizes modularity:

\begin{equation}
    Q = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{ij} \left[ A_{ij} - \frac{k_i k_j}{2m} \right] \delta(c_i, c_j)
\end{equation}

where $A_{ij}$ is the adjacency matrix, $k_i$ is node degree, $m$ is total edge weight, and $\delta(c_i, c_j) = 1$ if nodes $i$ and $j$ belong to the same community.

\subsubsection{Topic Labeling}

For each detected cluster, we extract characteristic terms using within-cluster TF-IDF:

\begin{equation}
    w_{t,c} = \text{tf}_{t,c} \times \log_2 \frac{|C|}{n_t^c}
\end{equation}

where $c$ indexes clusters and $n_t^c$ is the number of clusters containing term $t$. The top 5 terms by weight provide interpretable cluster labels.

\subsection{Transformer Embedding Approach}

\subsubsection{Sentence-BERT Embeddings}

We employ Sentence-BERT (SBERT) \cite{reimers2019sentence} to generate dense vector representations of venture descriptions.\marginnote{SBERT fine-tunes BERT for sentence-level tasks, producing embeddings where semantically similar sentences have high cosine similarity.} SBERT modifies the BERT architecture \cite{devlin2019bert} for efficient sentence-level similarity computation, producing 384-dimensional embeddings that capture semantic content.

We use the \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} model, which provides an effective balance between computational efficiency and semantic quality.\sidenote{The \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} model is trained on over 1 billion sentence pairs and achieves state-of-the-art performance on semantic similarity benchmarks.} For each venture description $d$, we obtain:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{e}_d = \text{SBERT}(d) \in \mathbb{R}^{384}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Dimensionality Reduction}

For visualization and clustering, we apply UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) \cite{mcinnes2018umap} to reduce embedding dimensionality while preserving local and global structure:\marginnote{UMAP preserves both local neighborhood structure and global topology better than alternatives like t-SNE, making it preferable for clustering applications.}

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_d = \text{UMAP}(\mathbf{e}_d) \in \mathbb{R}^{2}
\end{equation}

We use $n\_neighbors = 15$ and $min\_dist = 0.1$, following recommended defaults for clustering applications.

\subsubsection{Clustering}

We employ HDBSCAN (Hierarchical Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise) \cite{campello2013density} for cluster detection.\sidenote{HDBSCAN identifies clusters of varying density and explicitly handles noise points, making it more robust than DBSCAN or $k$-means for real-world data.} Unlike Louvain (which operates on network structure), HDBSCAN operates directly on the embedding space, identifying dense regions as clusters:

\begin{equation}
    \text{cluster}(d) = \text{HDBSCAN}(\mathbf{z}_d; \text{min\_cluster\_size} = 50)
\end{equation}

HDBSCAN returns soft cluster assignments (membership probabilities) and identifies outliers that do not belong to any cluster.

\subsubsection{Semantic Interpretation}

For cluster interpretation, we employ two complementary approaches:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Centroid analysis}: Computing cluster centroids in embedding space and identifying ventures closest to each centroid as exemplars
    \item \textbf{Keyword extraction}: Using KeyBERT \cite{grootendorst2020keybert} to extract semantically representative keywords from cluster members
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Structural Comparison}

To assess convergence between TF-IDF and embedding approaches, we compute:\marginnote{High correlation between methods would suggest robust structural findings; divergence would indicate that methods capture different aspects of positioning.}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Adjusted Rand Index (ARI)}: Measures agreement between cluster assignments, adjusted for chance
    \item \textbf{Normalized Mutual Information (NMI)}: Measures shared information between clusterings
    \item \textbf{Network correlation}: Spearman correlation between pairwise similarity matrices
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion: Positioning in Constructed Innovation Ecosystems}[Discussion]
\labsec{venture:discussion}

\subsection{Beyond Sectoral Categories}

Our findings challenge the assumption that innovation intermediary portfolios cluster neatly around sectoral mandates.\marginnote{The gap between mandate-derived categories and empirically observed positioning clusters suggests that strategic positioning follows logics distinct from formal organizational boundaries.} While KIC portfolios show expected sectoral concentration (HealthTech in EIT Health, CleanTech in Climate-KIC), the emergent cluster structure reveals positioning dimensions that transcend official categorization:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Business model positioning}: The Platform-SaaS cluster spans multiple KICs, indicating that platform business models constitute a cross-sectoral positioning orientation. Ventures position themselves as ``platforms'' regardless of whether they address health, climate, or food challenges.

    \item \textbf{Customer segment positioning}: B2B versus B2C orientations create positioning clusters that cross-cut sectoral boundaries. Enterprise-focused ventures from Digital, Health, and Manufacturing cluster together based on shared customer segment language.

    \item \textbf{Impact framing}: Sustainability and transformation narratives create positioning clusters orthogonal to technology categories. Ventures across KICs employ similar ``impact'' vocabulary, suggesting that mission-driven positioning is increasingly important for legitimacy.
\end{itemize}

These findings parallel \cite{basole2019visual}'s observation that positioning clusters in geographical ecosystems do not map to industry classifications. We extend this finding to institutionally constructed ecosystems, demonstrating that even deliberate sectoral mandates do not fully determine portfolio positioning structure.

\subsection{TF-IDF versus Transformer Approaches}

The comparison between TF-IDF and transformer embedding approaches reveals complementary strengths:\sidenote{Neither method is strictly superior; rather, they capture different aspects of positioning---keyword-based similarity versus semantic similarity.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{TF-IDF strengths}: Interpretability (cluster labels derive directly from keywords), computational efficiency, and comparability with prior research.

    \item \textbf{Transformer strengths}: Finer semantic granularity, ability to detect similarity despite vocabulary differences, and sensitivity to stylistic/narrative dimensions of positioning.

    \item \textbf{Complementarity}: Low cluster agreement (ARI = 0.042) combined with moderate similarity correlation indicates that methods capture related but structurally distinct patterns. Using both provides richer understanding than either alone.
\end{itemize}

For practitioners, this suggests that keyword-based analysis (accessible via standard tools) provides a useful first approximation, while transformer-based analysis reveals additional nuance for ventures whose positioning involves semantic sophistication beyond keyword choice.

\subsection{Implications for Innovation Intermediaries}

Our findings have implications for how innovation intermediaries understand and manage their portfolios:\marginnote{Portfolio positioning analysis can inform strategic decisions about venture selection, programme design, and ecosystem positioning.}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Portfolio positioning awareness}: Intermediaries should understand not only what sectors their portfolios address but how portfolio ventures position themselves strategically. A portfolio nominally focused on ``HealthTech'' may contain distinct sub-populations with different positioning strategies and competitive dynamics.

    \item \textbf{Cross-sectoral opportunity identification}: The presence of cross-cutting positioning clusters (Platform-SaaS, Impact-Sustainability) suggests opportunities for cross-KIC collaboration and learning. Ventures employing similar positioning strategies may benefit from shared programming regardless of sectoral focus.

    \item \textbf{Positioning gap analysis}: Comparing portfolio positioning against sector-wide positioning landscapes can reveal gaps---positioning orientations underrepresented in the portfolio relative to market opportunity.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Design Knowledge Contribution}
\labsec{venture:designknowledge}

Beyond its empirical scope, this chapter contributes three transferable design principles for cartographic workflows that operationalize enacted roles through investment and portfolio data:\marginnote{This chapter contributes the \textbf{enacted role} perspective---what organizations actually do with resources---complementing claimed roles (websites) and attributed roles (news media).}

\begin{description}
    \item[DP-C1: Dual representation pipeline for methodological triangulation.] Running TF-IDF (keyword-based) and transformer embedding (semantic) pipelines in parallel on the same corpus demonstrates that different analytical approaches surface different structural dimensions of the positioning landscape. TF-IDF captures explicit terminology clusters; transformer embeddings detect latent semantic similarity between ventures using different vocabulary for similar value propositions. For any text-heavy corpus, dual pipelines operationalize MDR-2 (theory-guided operationalization) by ensuring that both surface-level and deep semantic signals are systematically analyzed.

    \item[DP-C2: Enacted role operationalization through resource allocation data.] Crunchbase portfolio data operationalizes ``enacted roles'' through actual resource allocation patterns (funding received, investor relationships, organizational scale) rather than textual claims. This operationalizes MDR-1 (multi-source integration): combining enacted-role data with claimed-role data (websites, Ch.5) enables direct measurement of the claim-enactment gap---the systematic divergence between how organizations describe themselves and how they actually allocate resources. The gap is itself analytically productive.

    \item[DP-C3: Coverage profiling before focal subsetting.] Profiling the full Crunchbase universe (2.8M organizations, 48\% EIT-relevant) before selecting the EIT-affiliated subsample situates focal cases within their broader ecosystem context. This coverage mapping step operationalizes MDR-3 (scalable automation): automated sector classification at scale reveals ecosystem boundaries that would be invisible if analysis began with the focal subsample directly.
\end{description}

\begin{insightbox}[From Enacted Roles to Role Dynamics]
The Crunchbase analysis contributes the resource-flow dimension of multimodal cartography. While website analysis reveals \emph{claimed} roles and news media reveals \emph{attributed} roles, investment and positioning data reveal \emph{enacted} roles---what organizations actually do, whom they compete with, and how resources flow. The convergence or divergence between these views is analytically productive: ventures whose claimed positioning (websites) aligns with their enacted positioning (Crunchbase cluster membership) demonstrate role coherence; those with gaps reveal strategic ambiguity, aspiration, or repositioning attempts.
\end{insightbox}

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations qualify our findings:\sidenote{Limitations primarily concern data coverage and temporal scope; future work should address these through expanded data collection and longitudinal analysis.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Crunchbase coverage}: While comprehensive for technology ventures, Crunchbase may underrepresent certain venture types (e.g., hardware-focused, non-anglophone) and certain KICs (particularly newer ones).

    \item \textbf{Description quality}: Crunchbase descriptions are self-reported and variably maintained. Despite validation efforts, some descriptions may be outdated or strategically crafted rather than accurately descriptive.

    \item \textbf{Cross-sectional design}: Our analysis captures a single point in time. Positioning strategies evolve; longitudinal analysis would reveal how portfolio positioning changes as ecosystems mature.

    \item \textbf{Positioning versus performance}: We analyze positioning statements without linking to venture performance. Future research should examine whether positioning strategies predict funding success, survival, or growth.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cross-Modal Similarity Comparison}
\labsec{venture:similarity-comparison}

A methodologically significant extension addresses the question: \emph{How does Crunchbase-derived similarity compare to similarity derived from other data modalities?}\marginnote{This question operationalizes the dissertation's multimodal cartography framework: if different data sources reveal the same similarity structure, there may be a stable underlying ``role space''; if they diverge, each modality captures different facets of organizational roles.}

The \texttt{cross\_modal\_triangulation.py} module implements systematic comparison across three similarity structures:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0.5cm, itemsep=0.3em]
\item[Declared similarity (websites)] Organizations similar in how they describe themselves on websites. Captures self-presentation and claimed positioning.

\item[Attributed similarity (news)] Organizations similar in how media portrays them. Captures external perception and reputation.

\item[Enacted similarity (Crunchbase)] Organizations similar in investment profiles, funding patterns, and network position. Captures observable resource-flow behavior.
\end{description}

\noindent\textbf{Comparison metrics.} The module quantifies cross-modal agreement through:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.2em]
\item \textbf{Matrix correlation}: Pearson correlation between flattened similarity matrices reveals whether overall similarity structures align.
\item \textbf{Rank correlation}: Spearman correlation of similarity rankings provides ordinal comparison robust to outliers.
\item \textbf{Neighbor overlap}: Jaccard overlap in top-k nearest neighbors directly tests whether the same organizations cluster together across modalities.
\item \textbf{Divergent pairs}: Organization pairs with large similarity differences across modalities are analytically valuable---they reveal where modalities ``disagree'' about organizational proximity.
\end{itemize}

\begin{insightbox}[Research Opportunity: Similarity Structure Divergence]
If Company A and Company B appear highly similar in Crunchbase data (same investors, similar funding stage, co-investment network) but dissimilar in website text, this divergence is interpretable: they may compete for the same resources while positioning themselves differently to distinct audiences. Conversely, companies with similar website positioning but divergent enacted profiles may be aspirationally similar but operationally distinct. These patterns illuminate the strategic dynamics of positioning in innovation ecosystems.
\end{insightbox}

\noindent This comparison connects to the RoleSim integration documented in Section~\ref{sec:rolesim-crunchbase}: by applying influence typology to Crunchbase networks and comparing the resulting role classifications with roles derived from other modalities, we can assess whether ``role'' is a unitary concept or a multi-dimensional construct that depends on measurement perspective.

\section{Conclusion}
\labsec{venture:conclusion}

This chapter applied visual analytic methods to examine the structure of venture positioning across EIT Knowledge and Innovation Community portfolios.\marginnote{Our dual-method approach combining TF-IDF and transformer embeddings provides a template for future research on organizational positioning.} Analyzing 7,341 EIT-supported ventures from Crunchbase, we employed both traditional TF-IDF approaches (following \cite{basole2019visual}) and state-of-the-art transformer embeddings to reveal positioning structure within and across KIC portfolios.

Our findings reveal that venture positioning does not cluster neatly around KIC sectoral mandates. Instead, emergent clusters blend sectoral focus with cross-cutting themes---platform business models, sustainability narratives, customer segment orientations---that transcend official categorization. Transformer embeddings reveal finer-grained semantic structure than TF-IDF, particularly for distinguishing ventures with similar keywords but different underlying value propositions.

These findings contribute to understanding of both entrepreneurial ecosystems and innovation intermediary strategy. For ecosystem research, we demonstrate that text-based positioning analysis, pioneered for geographical ecosystems by \cite{basole2019visual}, extends productively to institutionally constructed ecosystems. For intermediary strategy, we show that portfolio composition reveals positioning structures that may diverge from stated mandates---structures that intermediaries should understand and intentionally manage.

Future research should extend this approach longitudinally, examining how portfolio positioning evolves as intermediaries and their ecosystems mature. Linking positioning analysis to performance outcomes would clarify whether particular positioning strategies predict venture success. And applying similar methods to other intermediary types (accelerators, technology transfer offices, corporate venture units) would reveal how positioning dynamics vary across the innovation intermediary landscape.
