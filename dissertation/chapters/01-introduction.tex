\begin{chapteroverview}
This dissertation begins with an open problem: sustainability transitions research cannot become a proper science without a shared, structured database of actors and their roles \parencite{dehaan2025makingscience}. Yet roles are not directly catalogable---they are enacted, contested, and distributed across digital modalities. The design challenge is therefore not simply \emph{what roles do intermediaries play?} but \emph{how do you design an infrastructure to find out?}

The answer is \textbf{multimodal cartography} and its product: the \textbf{Cartalog}---a cartographic catalog of actors enriched by seven digital modalities (websites, Twitter, Crunchbase, images, news, Wikipedia, video). Each modality contributes unique role signals through a designed workflow (Curation $\rightarrow$ Annotation $\rightarrow$ Unsupervised $\rightarrow$ Output); the workflows produce transferable design knowledge; their cumulative output is the Cartalog. Information Systems is uniquely placed at this intersection \parencite{maass2018data}: DSR supplies the design methodology, sociomateriality \parencite{Orlikowski2007} supplies the ontology, sustainability transitions supply the domain.

Three findings from the EIT demonstration case: a \emph{connector gap} between claimed and enacted bridging roles; a \emph{Knowledge Triangle imbalance} (business 49\%, research 37\%, education 14\%) challenging the EIT mandate; and \emph{five website sociotypes} invisible to single-modality analysis. This chapter establishes the catalog problem, frames the design response, introduces the Cartalog and \RoleBox{} as its instantiation, and maps the journey ahead.
\end{chapteroverview}


\section{The Urgency of Navigating Transitions}
\label{sec:intro:urgency}

We are living through what historians may one day call the Great Reckoning. Climate change has outpaced many scientific predictions \cite{ipcc2023ar6}.\sidenote{\textbf{420 ppm}---CO\textsubscript{2} levels in 2023, unseen for three million years.} Biodiversity loss continues at what some scientists characterize as a sixth mass extinction \cite{ipbes2019global}. Social inequalities have widened, exacerbated by technological disruption and pandemic aftershocks. These crises share a common origin: the unsustainability of systems that have dominated since the industrial revolution.

Addressing these challenges requires more than incremental fixes. We need \emph{transitions}---fundamental shifts in how societies produce energy, grow food, move people, and organize economic life. The shift from coal to renewables. From industrial to regenerative agriculture. From private cars to shared mobility. Each touches every dimension of socio-technical systems.

Over three decades, sustainability transitions research has developed sophisticated frameworks for understanding these transformations. The Multi-Level Perspective sees transitions emerging from interactions between niche innovations, dominant regimes, and broader landscape pressures \cite{geels2002technological}.\sidenote{The MLP remains the field's most influential framework, though it has faced critiques for underspecifying actor agency and micro-level dynamics.} Strategic Niche Management explores how protected spaces help radical innovations mature \cite{smith2007snm}. Transition Management investigates governance approaches for steering complex systems toward sustainability \cite{kemp2007tm}.

These frameworks have generated valuable insights. But the field faces a persistent problem---one that motivates this entire dissertation.

\section{The Methodological Divide}
\label{sec:intro:divide}

\subsection{The Qualitative Tradition}

Most transitions research is qualitative and case-based.\sidenote{\textbf{Designed data}---Groves (2011) distinguishes data created \emph{for} research (surveys, interviews) from data existing \emph{independently} of research (digital traces, transaction logs).} Researchers spend years embedded in transition arenas, conducting interviews, analyzing documents, observing how actors negotiate visions and navigate institutional barriers. This work excels at capturing complexity, contingency, and context. It reveals how actors make sense of change, how meanings shift, how power operates through discourse.

Consider the exemplary studies of the Dutch energy transition \cite{verbong2007dutch}, or the longitudinal analyses tracing organic agriculture's evolution from countercultural movement to mainstream practice \cite{smith2006organic}. These provide the theoretical depth that makes transitions research valuable.

But this tradition has limits.\sidenote{Qualitative strengths: deep context, rich meaning, process tracing, actor voice. Limits: small samples, hard to generalize, retrospective, hard to compare.} Case studies typically examine thirty to fifty actors---enough for rich understanding, not enough for pattern identification. By the time a transition is documented, it has largely already happened. Cross-case comparison remains challenging when contexts differ and operationalization varies.

\subsection{The Computational Turn}

Meanwhile, computational social science has developed powerful methods for analyzing large-scale social phenomena \cite{lazer2009computational,salganik2019bitbybit,grimmer2021mlsocialscience}. Network analysis reveals hidden collaborative structures \cite{newman2010networks,borgatti2009network}. Natural language processing extracts themes from millions of documents \cite{grimmer2013text}. Machine learning identifies patterns invisible to human observation \cite{molina2019machine}.

Applied to transitions, these methods promise to overcome scale limitations. Researchers have mapped innovation networks through patent analysis, tracked climate discourse dynamics on Twitter, and analyzed startup ecosystems at population level \cite{jacobides2018ecosystems,phillips2019innovation}. The potential is substantial.

Yet computational approaches face their own problems---particularly regarding theory \cite{ruths2014social,boyd2012critical}. Many studies begin with available data rather than theoretical questions, leading to sophisticated analysis of what \emph{can} be measured rather than what \emph{matters} \cite{tufekci2014big}. The rich concepts animating transitions research---intentionality, power, meaning, legitimacy, institutional work---often resist straightforward operationalization \cite{lawrence2009institutional}.\sidenote{\textbf{The data-driven call}---De Haan et al. (2019) argue for ``thorough-going empiricism'' where ``data, as many as can be mustered, lead rather than just support inference.''}

\subsection{Why Bridging Requires Design}

The divide between these approaches is not absolute\textemdash some researchers combine methods, some studies integrate data types. But such integration remains ad hoc rather than systematic, opportunistic rather than principled.

What is missing is explicit methodological work on \emph{how} to bridge these approaches while preserving each tradition's strengths. This dissertation argues that bridging requires treating integration as a \textbf{design problem}. Given particular materials (concepts, data, methods, expertise) and constraints (validity, resources, ethics), how can we construct artifacts enabling analysis that neither approach achieves alone?

This is where Design Science Research enters the picture. But before introducing that framework, we need to understand why role constellations make such a compelling test case for bridging work.


\section{Role Constellations: Central Yet Elusive}
\label{sec:intro:roles}

\subsection{What Are Role Constellations?}

Transitions unfold through the actions of diverse actors occupying different positions and performing different functions \cite{avelino2009power,fischer2016actors,laakso2024}. Some develop radical innovations in protected niches. Others maintain incumbent regimes. Still others broker connections, build coalitions, or provide legitimacy \cite{burt1992structural,granovetter1973strength}. Understanding these differentiated positions\textemdash what we call \emph{roles}\textemdash is essential for explaining why some transitions accelerate while others stall.\marginnote[0pt]{\footnotesize\textbf{Role}---A position or function enacted through relationships and activities, not fixed identity.}

But roles do not exist in isolation \cite{padgett2012emergence}. A niche innovation needs more than just entrepreneurs. It requires financiers willing to bet on uncertainty, brokers who can translate between worlds, legitimators who confer credibility, and sometimes even sympathetic regime insiders \cite{schot2018three,suchman1995managing}. Transitions depend not only on what actors do individually, but on how diverse roles configure together.

\begin{roledef}[Role Constellation]
A \textbf{role constellation} is the dynamic configuration of roles that an actor or set of actors performs, including how these roles relate to each other, evolve over time, and combine to enable (or constrain) transition processes.\marginnote[0pt]{\footnotesize\textbf{Foundational concept}---The term ``role constellation'' was introduced to transitions research by \textcite{wittmayer2017roles}, who defined it as ``webs of roles, which interact, interrelate and co-evolve with one another.'' This dissertation operationalizes their theoretical framework through multimodal cartography.}
\end{roledef}

The concept operates at two levels.\marginnote[0pt]{\footnotesize\textbf{Two levels}---(1) Multiple roles one actor performs, or (2) roles configured across a network. We focus on the first.} At the \emph{actor level}, a role constellation describes the portfolio of roles a single organization performs\textemdash how a university might simultaneously act as knowledge producer, network convenor, and policy advisor. At the \emph{system level}, it describes how different actors' roles combine into functional configurations that support innovation and change.

\subsection{Scope: Intermediaries and Their Roles}

Intermediary organizations occupy a special place in transitions \cite{kivimaa2019innovation,kanda2020conceptualising,vanlente2020intermediaries}.\marginnote[0pt]{\footnotesize\textbf{Intermediaries}---Organizations bridging innovators and regimes, translating between logics, navigating barriers.} Positioned between innovators and regimes, between sectors, between policy and practice, they perform critical bridging functions: facilitating connections, translating between logics, providing resources, conferring legitimacy, and helping innovations navigate institutional barriers.

This dissertation focuses specifically on \textbf{intermediary organizations and their role constellations}. We ask: what roles do intermediaries perform? How do these roles combine and evolve? What explains variation across intermediaries and over time?

\begin{importantbox}
\textbf{Scope and scalability.} While our empirical focus is intermediary organizations and the roles they perform, the methodological approach we develop\textemdash the analytical platform, integration protocols, and theoretical framework\textemdash scales to studying the broader networks that intermediaries mediate. We demonstrate feasibility at the intermediary level; extension to full ecosystem mapping is a logical next step.
\end{importantbox}

This scoping choice is strategic. Intermediaries are theoretically central to transitions yet understudied empirically \cite{howells2006intermediation,vanLente2003roles}. They leave substantial digital traces amenable to computational analysis \cite{salganik2019bitbybit}. And their bounded number (dozens to hundreds, not thousands) makes comprehensive analysis feasible while still requiring methods that go beyond traditional case studies.

\subsection{Six Problems in Current Research}

Despite the theoretical importance of roles, existing research faces six interconnected problems. These are not fatal flaws but productive tensions pointing toward opportunities for development.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.5em]

\item \textbf{Definitional ambiguity.} The term ``role'' means different things to different scholars: position, function, identity, category, or behavior. Are roles what actors \emph{do}, who actors \emph{are}, or where actors are \emph{positioned}? This conceptual slippage makes comparison and accumulation difficult.

\item \textbf{Static categorization.} Most typologies present roles as fixed boxes\textemdash entrepreneur, incumbent, intermediary\textemdash that actors occupy permanently. Yet empirical observation suggests otherwise. Actors shift roles over time, occupy multiple roles simultaneously, and vary their role performance by context. Start-ups become incumbents. Regime actors launch niche innovations. Static typologies cannot capture this dynamism.

\item \textbf{Actor-centric bias.} Roles are typically treated as attributes of individual actors: this organization \emph{is} an intermediary, that firm \emph{is} an incumbent. But relational approaches suggest roles emerge through interactions and network positions. An intermediary is only an intermediary \emph{in relation to} others it connects.

\item \textbf{Normative loading.} Role concepts often carry implicit value judgments\textemdash intermediaries presumed beneficial, incumbents framed as barriers. These judgments may sometimes be warranted, but they should be examined explicitly rather than smuggled in through terminology.

\item \textbf{Methodological constraints.} The small-N qualitative methods dominating transitions research enable rich understanding but struggle with population-level questions.\marginnote[0pt]{\footnotesize\textbf{The numbers}---82\% qualitative methods, 58\% interviews, only 9\% quantitative \parencite{zolfagharian2019studying}.} How common is role multiplicity? Do particular constellations correlate with transition success? How do constellations evolve across transition phases?

\item \textbf{Theoretical fragmentation.} Different transitions frameworks emphasize different roles. The Multi-Level Perspective focuses on niche-regime dynamics. Strategic Niche Management highlights innovation champions. Transition Management emphasizes frontrunners and change agents. These perspectives complement rather than compete, yet the field lacks an integrative framework.

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The Catalog Problem}

Running through these six problems is a deeper challenge that \textcite{dehaan2025makingscience} identifies as the central obstacle to cumulative transitions science: \textbf{there is no shared database of actors and their roles}.\sidenote{\textcite{dehaan2025makingscience} frames this as the prerequisite for ``making it a science'': a collective, structured, and interoperable actor database that allows the field to accumulate role knowledge across studies, contexts, and time.} Qualitative research embeds actor descriptions in case narratives---not shareable, queryable records. Computational studies produce datasets tailored to specific research questions---not interoperable catalogs. Each study re-invents the wheel. The field's knowledge of \emph{who does what} in sustainability transitions is rich but fragmented, unreplicable, and non-cumulative.

De Haan's prescription is a shared actor database: a structured, collectively maintained catalog that accumulates evidence about roles from every available source. But building such a catalog faces a fundamental design obstacle: \textbf{roles are not directly observable}. You cannot survey an actor and ask for its roles---self-reports are strategic and institutionally constrained. There is no official register of transition roles---they emerge from relationships, activities, and recognition by others. Roles must be \emph{inferred} from multiple weak signals, triangulated across sources, and updated as actors evolve.

This is the \textbf{catalog problem} this dissertation addresses through design. The designed answer is the \textbf{Cartalog}\textemdash a \emph{cart}ographic cata\emph{log}: a rich, multimodally-enriched database of actors in which each entity accumulates role signals from every modality that has observed it.\marginnote{Portmanteau of \textbf{cartography} (the practice of mapping) and \textbf{catalog} (the structured record). The name encodes the method: you cannot directly catalog roles, so you map them instead, and the accumulated maps \emph{become} the catalog.} Building the Cartalog requires designing four interdependent workflow layers for each modality: \textbf{curation} (surveying the territory), \textbf{processing} (map-making from raw traces), \textbf{archiving} (preserving surveys and maps for reuse), and \textbf{catalog assembly} (enriching each actor's entry with the modality's contribution). This dissertation designs those layers and demonstrates them on the EIT ecosystem.

\subsection{The Promise of Systematic Integration}

These problems\textemdash the six theoretical ones and the catalog problem that underlies them\textemdash point toward a common opportunity. If roles could be analyzed computationally at population scale \emph{while} retaining sensitivity to their emergent, relational, and dynamic properties, and if the resulting knowledge could be accumulated in a shared, structured database, new insights might emerge that neither qualitative nor computational traditions could generate alone. Patterns invisible in small samples could become apparent. The Cartalog could grow, modality by modality, study by study, into the shared infrastructure the field needs.

This dissertation proposes \textbf{multimodal cartography} as the methodology for building the Cartalog. The cartographic framing is not metaphorical---it is literal. Cartography as a practice has always involved the same four activities: surveying (curation), projection (processing), archiving (data preservation), and atlas-making (catalog assembly). Each activity requires designed instruments. Each instrument has unique affordances, limitations, and distortions. No single instrument reveals the full territory. The \emph{atlas} is what you get when you overlay all the maps. Mapping role constellations requires integrating multiple data modalities\textemdash what organizations say on their websites, how they interact on social media, how media portrays them, what their visual communications convey, how they are classified by investors, how Wikipedia editors categorize them, how they move and coordinate on video\textemdash into the cumulative, cross-modal Cartalog.

\begin{roledef}[Multimodal Cartography]
\textbf{Multimodal cartography} is a Design Science methodology for building a Cartalog: it integrates multiple data modalities (text, networks, images, video, structured databases) through designed curation, processing, and archiving workflows to construct a systematically enriched actor database---in this case, mapping the role constellations of intermediary organizations in sustainability transitions.
\end{roledef}

The ``multimodal'' dimension is essential.\marginnote[0pt]{\footnotesize\textbf{Why multimodal?}---Organizations \emph{claim} roles on websites, \emph{perform} them on Twitter, and are \emph{perceived} differently in media. Rather than imposing predetermined categories, each modality was approached with openness---letting sociotypes, gaps, and imbalances emerge inductively.} Roles are not singular phenomena observable through any single lens. An intermediary may claim a convening role on its website, perform a brokering role through its Twitter interactions, and be perceived as an advocate in news coverage. These are not contradictions to be resolved but facets to be mapped. Single-modality analysis\textemdash whether text-only, network-only, or interview-only\textemdash inevitably misses dimensions that other modalities would reveal.

The ultimate design challenge this dissertation addresses is therefore infrastructural: \textbf{how do you build open exchange on roles in sustainability transitions research?} The name of the platform encodes the answer: \RoleBox{}---an open infrastructure for exchanging knowledge about organizational roles. \textcite{dehaan2025makingscience} identifies the shared database as the prerequisite; \textcite{maass2018data} provides the IS framework for bridging data-driven and theory-driven approaches; \textcite{miranda2022computationally} names the genre---\emph{computationally intensive theory construction}---as a legitimate mode of IS contribution in which computational methods serve not just analysis but the construction of transferable theoretical knowledge.\sidenote{\textcite{miranda2022computationally} define computationally intensive theory construction as ``using computationally intensive approaches in the service of building new theory''---producing theoretical insights from patterns identified using computational techniques, including those that reveal categories, associations, networks, sequences, and complex system dynamics.} Together, these three papers frame what \RoleBox{} does: it designs the Cartalog and its seven modality workflows as open infrastructure for community-wide role exchange. The EIT ecosystem is the demonstration context, not the scope. When another researcher applies a RoleBox workflow to a different transition---say, urban mobility or agri-food---their actor entries enrich the same shared Cartalog. That is when the design works. That is the accumulated validation that transforms a demonstrably feasible artifact into scientifically reliable design knowledge \parencite{vombrocke2020accumulation}.

Open exchange, however, must be distinguished from open science. \textbf{Open science} is about reproducibility: making data, code, and results publicly accessible so others can verify findings \parencite{nosek2015promoting}. \textbf{Open exchange} goes further: it is about \emph{cumulativity}---building a shared infrastructure through which a community collectively constructs and tests theory across contexts and over time.\sidenote{The distinction maps onto de Haan's two-stage vision: open science is the prerequisite (shareable, standardised data); open exchange is the goal (a living shared database that grows with every new study). One can practice open science without contributing to cumulative knowledge; open exchange requires designed infrastructure that persists across individual studies.} Open science asks: can others reproduce your findings? Open exchange asks: can others \emph{extend} the Cartalog, contribute their transition context's actors, and have those entries speak to the same theoretical questions? This requires more than FAIR data principles---it requires a shared theoretical language, shared workflow protocols, and shared database infrastructure. It is, in this sense, closer to what \textcite{dehaan2025makingscience} means by ``making it a science'' than to what open access mandates require.

But open exchange is not an end in itself---it is a means to theory construction. This is where role theory and complex adaptive systems (CAS) enter as the dissertation's second intellectual engine. \textcite{wittmayer2017roles} theorizes not only what roles \emph{are} but what \emph{role constellations} are: ``webs of roles, which interact, interrelate and co-evolve with one another''---configurations in which the presence, absence, or transformation of one role reshapes the functional possibilities of all others. \textcite{avelino2014shifting} adds the power dimension: role constellations are sites of contestation, where shifting relations between empowered and disempowered actors drive or stall transitions. Together they provide a rich theoretical vocabulary---but one that cannot, on its own, generate predictions about system-level dynamics. What happens when connector roles are structurally absent? When regime actors colonize niche functions? When a role constellation reaches a tipping point? These are questions for complex adaptive systems \parencite{geels2019typology,levin1998ecosystems}. Agent-based models can operationalize role-theoretic propositions as behavioral rules, simulate the emergent configurations that result, and generate hypotheses that no amount of qualitative fieldwork could produce.\sidenote{This is the function of \textbf{RoleSim}, the agent-based complement to RoleBox introduced in Intermezzo~B: not a descriptive model of EIT, but a generative hypothesis engine for role constellation dynamics in transitions.} The Cartalog then provides the empirical surface against which those hypotheses are tested: does the real EIT ecosystem exhibit the configurations CAS predicts? Where it matches, theory is corroborated; where it diverges, new propositions emerge.

This is the theory--test loop that \textcite{miranda2022computationally} describe as computationally intensive theory construction---and it requires all three elements to function. Without the Cartalog there is no empirical surface; without CAS there are no system-level hypotheses worth testing; without the open exchange infrastructure there is no accumulation across studies. \RoleBox{} is designed to close this loop, and each modality chapter contributes one \textbf{cartographic workflow}---the designed procedure through which a single digital modality's traces become Cartalog entries and, ultimately, theoretical evidence.

This dissertation develops and demonstrates \RoleBox{} as that open exchange infrastructure, instantiating seven data modalities in an integrated analytical environment built around the Cartalog. But before detailing the artifacts, we must establish the methodological framework that guides their construction: Design Science Research.


\section{Design Science Research as Framework}
\label{sec:intro:dsr}

\subsection{Why Design?}

The challenge of bridging methodological divides is not primarily a matter of applying existing methods more skillfully or collecting more data. Rather, it requires \emph{creating} new methodological artifacts\textemdash conceptual frameworks, analytical tools, integration protocols, validation procedures\textemdash that enable forms of analysis not previously possible.

This is fundamentally a design problem \cite{simon1996sciences}. Engineers designing a bridge must understand materials, forces, and contexts. Similarly, bridging methodological divides requires systematic attention to what each approach offers, where tensions arise, and how artifacts can be constructed that enable productive integration. The question shifts from ``which method is better?'' to ``what can we build that serves both traditions?''

\subsection{Information Systems as Metatheoretical Paradigm}

Design Science Research does not float free.\marginnote[0pt]{\footnotesize\textbf{IS origins}---DSR emerged in Information Systems \parencite{hevner2004design} as a way to theorise the construction and evaluation of IT artifacts. This dissertation returns DSR to its IS roots---and extends them.} It originates in Information Systems \cite{hevner2004design,peffers2007dsr}, where the study of designed artifacts has always been entangled with the material conditions of their deployment. This dissertation returns DSR to its IS roots by adopting a \textbf{sociomaterial stance}: the computational workflows we design are not neutral instruments operating on pre-given data but \emph{sociomaterial assemblages} that co-produce the role knowledge they generate \cite{Orlikowski2007,orlikowski2008sociomateriality}.

This stance matters for two reasons. First, the digital platforms from which we draw our data---websites, Twitter/X, Crunchbase, news archives, visual media---are themselves material infrastructures with specific affordances that shape what kinds of organisational roles become observable \cite{leonardi2012materiality,kallinikos2013ambivalent}. A website affords editorial self-description; Twitter affords relational classification; Crunchbase affords financial categorisation. These are not neutral recording devices but co-constitutive of the phenomena they make visible. When we observe different role profiles across modalities, this divergence is not merely methodological noise---it is an ontological feature of the sociomaterial world that Information Systems is uniquely equipped to theorise.

Second, sustainability transitions and digital transformation are themselves constitutively entangled \cite{owoyele2025datacentering}. The data centres, cloud platforms, and algorithmic infrastructures through which contemporary organisations perform their roles---and through which researchers observe those performances---consume over 400 TWh of electricity annually, making them simultaneously infrastructure \emph{for} transitions and infrastructure \emph{requiring} transition.\marginnote[0pt]{\footnotesize\textbf{Entangled transitions}---Studying sustainability transitions \emph{through} digital infrastructure that itself \emph{requires} sustainability transition is the distinctive reflexive condition of contemporary computational social science \parencite{owoyele2025datacentering}.} Treating computational methods as merely instrumental---as transparent tools applied to a pre-given reality---obscures this entanglement. Information Systems provides the metatheoretical paradigm that makes it visible: DSR supplies the methodology for artifact construction; sociomateriality supplies the ontology; sustainability transitions supply the domain.

This positioning has consequences for every element of the dissertation. The Meta-Design Requirements we develop in Chapter~\ref{ch:method} are not merely functional specifications but sociomaterial requirements specifying how data modalities and theoretical constructs must be reconciled through designed artifacts. The datasets we curate are not raw material but digitally mediated traces shaped by platform architectures. The cross-modal divergence patterns we discover in Chapters~\ref{ch:interstitial}--\ref{ch:power} are not measurement limitations but analytically productive consequences of platform materiality. And the design knowledge we distil in Chapter~\ref{ch:synthesis} is IS design knowledge in the full sense---transferable insights about building sociomaterial artifacts for complex social phenomena.

\subsection{Design Science in Sustainability Transitions: Uncharted Territory}

Design Science Research (DSR) provides a systematic framework for this kind of methodological innovation. \sidecite{hevner2004design} Originating in information systems research \cite{march1995design}, DSR treats the creation and evaluation of novel artifacts as legitimate scientific contribution \cite{peffers2007dsr}. Unlike explanatory research that seeks to understand existing phenomena, or critical research that interrogates power and assumptions, design research asks: \emph{what new capabilities become possible through systematic artifact creation?}

Yet DSR remains largely uncharted territory in sustainability transitions research.\marginnote[0pt]{\footnotesize\textbf{The artifact gap}---Transitions research excels at theory but rarely produces reusable tools or datasets.} The field has produced sophisticated theoretical frameworks\textemdash the Multi-Level Perspective, Strategic Niche Management, Transition Management\textemdash but has devoted far less attention to the \emph{artifacts} that enable empirical analysis.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.3em]
\item \textbf{Analytical platforms} with documented codebases that others can deploy
\item \textbf{Curated datasets} with clear provenance and quality documentation
\item \textbf{Computational workflows} specifying reproducible analytical steps
\item \textbf{User interfaces} enabling researchers without programming expertise to conduct analyses
\item \textbf{Integration protocols} detailing how to combine different data sources and methods
\item \textbf{Methodological guidelines} translating tacit expertise into transferable procedures
\end{itemize}

This absence is consequential. Without such artifacts, each research team starts from scratch. Findings cannot be replicated or extended. Methodological innovations remain locked in individual projects rather than accumulating as shared infrastructure. The field advances through theoretical refinement but lacks the material foundations for cumulative empirical progress.\marginnote[0pt]{\footnotesize\textbf{De Haan et al. (2019)}---``Not only is there no such database but moreover it seems contrary to the way data are used in transitions research.''}

This dissertation imports the Design Science paradigm from information systems to address this gap. The contribution is not merely to \emph{use} computational methods but to \emph{design, document, and share} the artifacts that make multimodal cartography accessible and reproducible.

\subsection{Guiding Questions}

The overarching question guiding this dissertation is:

\begin{mainrq}
How should data-driven, theory-guided workflows be designed to enable multimodal cartography of interstitial role constellations in sustainability transitions?
\end{mainrq}

This question combines theoretical, methodological, empirical, and practical ambitions. It asks how to \emph{think about} role constellations, how to \emph{design} workflows that map them systematically, what patterns \emph{emerge} when applied, and what this \emph{implies} for cumulative research. Four sub-questions structure the inquiry:

\begin{subrq}
\textbf{Theory}\\
What conceptual framework enables theory-guided but empirically open analysis of role constellations?
\end{subrq}

\begin{subrq}
\textbf{Method}\\
What methodology enables systematic cross-modal mapping of claimed, attributed, and enacted roles?
\end{subrq}

\begin{subrq}
\textbf{Empirics}\\
What role constellation patterns characterize interstitial actors in the EIT ecosystem?
\end{subrq}

\begin{subrq}
\textbf{Implications}\\
How can design science workflows \emph{break down} open science barriers in role research while \emph{building up} infrastructure for cumulative knowledge accumulation?
\end{subrq}

\textbf{Theoretical contributions} advance how we conceptualize role constellations in sustainability transitions. The CAS reconceptualization offers mechanisms\textemdash emergence, self-organization, co-evolution, non-linearity\textemdash that explain constellation dynamics while enabling operationalization, expressed as 15 testable propositions.

\textbf{Methodological contributions} demonstrate that Design Science Research can productively guide social science methodology development. The four Meta-Design Requirements provide a transferable framework for bridging data-driven and theory-driven approaches. Critically, this dissertation responds to calls for ``data-driven transitions research'' by demonstrating how \emph{multimodal organic data}---digital traces generated independently of research objectives, integrated across five modalities---can substitute for the \emph{designed data} (interviews, surveys) that dominates the field. Beyond artifacts, the dissertation articulates \emph{design knowledge}---seven design principles, five design patterns, and practitioner guidelines that transfer to other multimodal research contexts.

\textbf{Empirical contributions} demonstrate the platform's analytical potential through exploratory application to eight intermediation domains across 2,192 organizations and 14 years. Key patterns include the \emph{connector gap} (aspiration-reality divergence), \emph{knowledge triangle imbalance} (business dominance), and \emph{five recurring sociotypes} that emerge across domains.

\textbf{Infrastructural contributions} create the foundations for cumulative knowledge development. \RoleBox{} provides an open-source platform with six documented computational workflows, each with modality-specific data pipelines, interactive visualization UIs, and systematic annotation guidelines. \textbf{CARTALOG}, the master organizational catalog, links 8,600+ organizations across all data modalities---enabling researchers to trace how the same organization is portrayed across websites, social media, news, and visual materials.


\section{Scope and Boundaries}
\label{sec:intro:scope}

Clarity requires specifying not only what this dissertation attempts but also what it does not.

\textbf{Empirically}, the dissertation focuses on European Institute of Innovation and Technology (EIT) Knowledge and Innovation Communities as the primary case.\marginnote[0pt]{\footnotesize Eight EIT KICs, 2010--2023. European innovation intermediaries.} This choice enables comparison across sustainability domains with consistent institutional framing and substantial digital traces.

\textbf{Methodologically}, the heavy reliance on digital trace data privileges what is observable online.\marginnote[0pt]{\footnotesize Digital traces only; offline activities not captured.} Organizations with limited digital presence, interactions through non-digital channels, and dimensions leaving few digital traces are underrepresented or invisible.

\textbf{Theoretically}, the dissertation focuses on micro-level role constellation dynamics rather than macro-level regime change. It treats landscape pressures and regime structures as context rather than primary objects of study.

\textbf{Normatively}, the dissertation is primarily descriptive and analytical rather than prescriptive. While occasional normative questions arise, the aim is understanding how role constellations emerge and evolve, not prescribing ideal configurations or governance interventions.

\begin{importantbox}
\textbf{What this dissertation is not:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.2em]
\item A comprehensive theory of sustainability transitions
\item A normative framework for evaluating transition progress
\item A technical report on machine learning innovations
\item A celebration of big data over traditional methods
\item A finished methodology for mechanical adoption elsewhere
\end{itemize}
\end{importantbox}

\subsection{Reading This Dissertation}

The chapters present not a smooth narrative where each step follows logically from the last, but an honest account of iterative refinement through partial success and instructive failure. They show both what multimodal cartography enables and what limitations persist despite best efforts. They offer not a finished product but infrastructure that others can build upon, critique, and adapt.

The urgency of sustainability transitions demands that researchers continually expand their methodological repertoires while maintaining theoretical rigor and reflexive honesty. This dissertation contributes one set of tools, one theoretical lens, one empirical demonstration, and one open invitation. The maps we draw are never the territory. But better maps might help us navigate the territory more wisely.

The journey begins with an intermezzo introducing the EIT ecosystem, then proceeds to Chapter~\ref{ch:background}, where we survey the territory itself: sustainability transitions, the actors who shape them, and the roles those actors play.
