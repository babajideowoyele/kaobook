\section{Introduction: Who Does What to Whom in Innovation Ecosystems?}
\labsec{power:intro}

How do innovation intermediaries linguistically construct power relations among ecosystem actors?\marginnote{Power relations in innovation ecosystems shape resource allocation, decision-making authority, and whose voices are heard in sustainability transitions \needcite{avelino-power-2017}.} When the European Institute of Innovation and Technology (EIT) describes how ``Climate-KIC \textit{accelerates} startups'' or how ``entrepreneurs \textit{transform} industries,'' these verb choices encode implicit claims about who holds power, who exercises agency, and who is acted upon. Such linguistic framings are not neutral descriptions but active constructions that shape how stakeholders understand their roles within innovation ecosystems and, ultimately, who benefits from sustainability transitions.

This chapter applies verb-based connotation frame analysis to examine power and agency dynamics in EIT Knowledge and Innovation Community (KIC) communications.\sidenote{Connotation frames capture the implied social dynamics encoded in verb predicates---who has power over whom, who exercises agency, and how actors are valued \needcite{rashkin-connotation-2016} \needcite{sap-connotation-2017}.} Building on the RIVETER framework developed by Antoniak et al. \needcite{antoniak-riveter-2023}, we extract Agent-Verb-Theme triples from EIT discourse to measure how different actors---entrepreneurs, students, researchers, corporations, policymakers, and the KICs themselves---are positioned in power hierarchies and attributed agency. This approach moves beyond analyzing \textit{what} is said to examine \textit{how} it is said: the grammatical and semantic structures through which social relations are encoded.

The EIT ecosystem provides an ideal setting for examining power dynamics in sustainability transitions discourse.\marginnote{The EIT's mandate to integrate education, research, and business brings together actors with historically different power positions and institutional logics.} The nine KICs---Climate-KIC, EIT Digital, InnoEnergy, EIT Health, RawMaterials, EIT Food, EIT Manufacturing, EIT Urban Mobility, and EIT Culture \& Creativity---collectively address major societal challenges through innovation. Their communications construct narratives about transformation, disruption, and system change that necessarily position some actors as agents of change and others as objects of intervention. Understanding these constructions matters because they shape expectations, legitimacy, and ultimately the distribution of benefits from innovation-driven transitions.

Our analysis addresses three research questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{RQ1}: How are different actor types (entrepreneurs, students, researchers, corporations, KICs) positioned in terms of power and agency in EIT discourse?
    \item \textbf{RQ2}: How do power and agency framings vary across KICs addressing different sustainability challenges?
    \item \textbf{RQ3}: What verb patterns characterize high-power versus low-power actor positions, and what do these reveal about implicit assumptions in innovation ecosystem discourse?
\end{enumerate}

Our findings reveal systematic asymmetries in how EIT discourse constructs power relations. Entrepreneurs and KICs themselves are consistently framed with high agency and power; students and ``society'' are more frequently positioned as beneficiaries or objects of action; researchers occupy an ambiguous middle position. These patterns persist across KICs but vary in intensity: Climate-KIC discourse emphasizes transformation and system change with corresponding power differentials; EIT Digital emphasizes enabling and connecting with more egalitarian framings. Crucially, the actors positioned as beneficiaries of sustainability transitions---citizens, communities, future generations---are rarely constructed as agents in their own right.

\section{Background: Verb Predicates, Power, and Agency}
\labsec{power:background}

\subsection{Connotation Frames: Capturing Implied Social Dynamics}

Understanding who does what to whom has been central to linguistics since P\=a\d{n}ini's grammar in the 4th century BCE.\marginnote{Frame semantics argues that understanding a sentence involves knowledge evoked by concepts in the sentence, not just denotational meaning \needcite{fillmore-frame-1976}.} Modern approaches to semantic role labeling categorize how words relate to verb predicates: the \textsc{Agent} causes an event; the \textsc{Theme} is most directly affected by it \needcite{gildea-automatic-2000}. But beyond these structural roles, verbs carry \textit{connotative} meanings---implied social dynamics that shape interpretation.

Rashkin et al. \needcite{rashkin-connotation-2016} introduced \textit{connotation frames} to model these implied meanings.\sidenote{Connotation frames were first applied to analyze political bias in news articles and have since been used across diverse domains including film, social media, textbooks, and Wikipedia \needcite{antoniak-riveter-2023}.} For any verb predicate $v$ with Agent $a$ and Theme $t$, connotation frames capture:

\begin{description}
    \item[Effect] Whether $v$ has positive or negative effect on $a$ or $t$
    \item[Value] Whether $a$ or $t$ are presupposed to have value
    \item[State] The likely mental state of $a$ or $t$ as result of $v$
    \item[Perspective] Sentiment relations between $a$ and $t$, and how writer/reader perceive them
\end{description}

Sap et al. \needcite{sap-connotation-2017} extended this framework with \textit{power} and \textit{agency} dimensions:

\begin{description}
    \item[Power] The power differential between Agent and Theme. When someone ``\textit{traps}'' another, the Agent has power over the Theme.
    \item[Agency] Whether the Agent is decisive and can act upon their environment. ``\textit{Slicing}'' connotes high agency; ``\textit{obeying}'' connotes low agency.
\end{description}

We extend this line of research to innovation ecosystem discourse, examining how EIT communications construct power relations among ecosystem actors.

\subsection{Power in Sustainability Transitions}

Sustainability transitions research has increasingly recognized power as central to understanding how---and for whom---transitions unfold \cite{avelino2017power, meadowcroft2009politics}.\marginnote{``Transitions are not just technical or economic processes; they are deeply political processes involving struggles over the direction of change'' \cite{meadowcroft2009politics}.} Transitions involve not merely technological substitution but reconfigurations of actor relationships, institutional arrangements, and distributions of costs and benefits.

\cite{avelino2017power} distinguish multiple forms of power relevant to transitions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reinforcive power}: Capacity to reinforce existing structures
    \item \textbf{Innovative power}: Capacity to create new resources and structures
    \item \textbf{Transformative power}: Capacity to fundamentally alter distributions
\end{itemize}

Innovation intermediaries like EIT KICs occupy ambiguous positions in these power dynamics.\sidenote{Intermediaries may either reinforce existing power structures by privileging incumbent actors or enable transformative power by supporting challengers and outsiders.} They possess resources (funding, networks, legitimacy) that they distribute to selected actors; their selections implicitly construct hierarchies of worthiness and capability. How intermediaries \textit{linguistically} construct these relations---who is framed as having power and agency---both reflects and shapes the actual distribution of transition benefits.

\subsection{Linguistic Framing of Innovation}

Research on innovation discourse has documented how language shapes perceptions of technological change.\marginnote{Innovation discourse typically employs heroic narratives featuring entrepreneurial protagonists who overcome obstacles to deliver progress \cite{pfotenhauer2019politics}.} Terms like ``disruption,'' ``transformation,'' and ``revolution'' carry implicit assumptions about the desirability and inevitability of change \cite{vinsel2021innovation}. Such framings privilege certain actors (disruptors, innovators, entrepreneurs) while marginalizing others (incumbents, traditional practitioners, affected communities).

Critical discourse analysis has examined how innovation policy constructs subject positions:\sidenote{Subject positions are the identities and roles that discourse makes available to actors---who can speak, act, and be heard \cite{fairclough2013critical}.}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \cite{jessop2013cultural} analyzes how ``knowledge-based economy'' discourse positions knowledge workers as agents and others as objects of restructuring
    \item \cite{tyfield2012transition} examines how ``green growth'' narratives maintain power asymmetries while appearing transformative
    \item \cite{swyngedouw2010impossible} critiques how sustainability discourse depoliticizes by framing environmental challenges as technical rather than political
\end{itemize}

Our approach complements this qualitative tradition with systematic, corpus-based analysis of verb connotations---enabling examination of implicit power constructions at scale while preserving interpretability.

\section{Methods: Extracting Power and Agency from EIT Discourse}
\labsec{power:methods}

\subsection{Overview: The RIVETER Pipeline}

We adapt the RIVETER framework \cite{antoniak2023riveter} for analysis of EIT communications.\marginnote{RIVETER provides a complete pipeline for analyzing verb connotations associated with entities, including parsing, entity recognition, coreference resolution, and lexicon matching.} The pipeline proceeds in five stages:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Document parsing}: Tokenization, sentence segmentation, part-of-speech tagging
    \item \textbf{Entity recognition}: Identification of people, organizations, and actor categories
    \item \textbf{Coreference resolution}: Linking pronouns and references to canonical entities
    \item \textbf{Triple extraction}: Identifying Agent-Verb-Theme relations via dependency parsing
    \item \textbf{Lexicon matching}: Scoring verbs for power and agency dimensions
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Corpus Construction}

We constructed a corpus from EIT KIC public communications spanning 2015--2024:\sidenote{The corpus emphasizes strategic and promotional communications where power framing is most salient; technical reports and financial documents were excluded.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Website content}: Main pages, programme descriptions, news articles
    \item \textbf{Strategic documents}: Annual reports, impact reports, strategy documents
    \item \textbf{Promotional materials}: Brochures, press releases, social media posts
    \item \textbf{Ecosystem narratives}: Startup profiles, alumni stories, partner descriptions
\end{itemize}

Table \ref{tab:corpus-overview-power} summarizes the corpus. After preprocessing (removing boilerplate, deduplication), the corpus comprises 9,423 documents containing 231,816 sentences.

\subsection{Entity Recognition and Classification}

Standard named entity recognition (NER) identifies specific people and organizations, but our research questions require analysis of \textit{actor categories}---classes of actors that recur across documents.\marginnote{Actor categories like ``entrepreneurs'' or ``students'' appear far more frequently than specific named individuals and are more analytically tractable for examining systematic power patterns.} We developed a custom entity recognition layer combining:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{spaCy NER}: For named entities (people, organizations)
    \item \textbf{Custom gazetteer}: For actor category terms
    \item \textbf{Coreference resolution}: For linking pronouns and references
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Coreference Resolution}

We employ neuralcoref (spaCy add-on) to link pronouns and nominal references to their antecedents.\sidenote{Coreference resolution is essential because many power-relevant verbs appear with pronominal subjects or objects: ``They \textit{accelerate} startups''; ``We \textit{support} them.''} When coreference chains include both named entities and category terms, we assign the chain to the most specific category.

\subsection{Triple Extraction}

Using spaCy's dependency parser, we extract Agent-Verb-Theme triples:\marginnote{Dependency parsing identifies grammatical relations: \texttt{nsubj} (nominal subject, typically Agent) and \texttt{dobj} (direct object, typically Theme).}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Identify all verbs in the sentence
    \item Extract the \texttt{nsubj} (nominal subject) $\rightarrow$ Agent
    \item Extract the \texttt{dobj} (direct object) $\rightarrow$ Theme
    \item Lemmatize verb for lexicon matching
    \item Link Agent/Theme spans to entity categories
\end{enumerate}

Not all verbs have both Agent and Theme (intransitive verbs, passives); we retain triples with at least an Agent for agency analysis, requiring both for power analysis.

\subsubsection{Example Extraction}

Consider the sentence: ``\textit{Climate-KIC accelerates promising startups across Europe.}''

This triple is then matched against the connotation frames lexicon.

\subsection{Connotation Frame Lexicon}

We employ the power and agency lexicon from \cite{sap2017connotation}, which contains 1,700+ verbs annotated for:\sidenote{Annotations were collected via crowdsourcing using Agent-Theme placeholder prompts (``X [verb]s Y''); see \cite{sap2017connotation} for annotation methodology.}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Agency(a)}: Agent's agency level ($+1$, $0$, $-1$)
    \item \textbf{Power(a)}: Agent's power relative to Theme ($+1$, $0$, $-1$)
    \item \textbf{Power(t)}: Theme's power relative to Agent ($+1$, $0$, $-1$)
\end{itemize}

For each extracted triple, we look up the lemmatized verb and retrieve its connotation scores. Unmatched verbs (not in lexicon) are excluded from scoring.

\subsection{Score Aggregation}

For each actor category $e$, we aggregate scores across all triples where $e$ appears as Agent or Theme:\marginnote{Mean scores enable comparison across categories with different frequencies; we also report medians and distributions to capture heterogeneity.}

\begin{equation}
    \text{Agency}(e) = \frac{1}{|T_e^{agent}|} \sum_{(e, v, t) \in T_e^{agent}} \text{Agency}(v)
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \text{Power}_{agent}(e) = \frac{1}{|T_e^{agent}|} \sum_{(e, v, t) \in T_e^{agent}} \text{Power}_a(v)
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \text{Power}_{theme}(e) = \frac{1}{|T_e^{theme}|} \sum_{(a, v, e) \in T_e^{theme}} \text{Power}_t(v)
\end{equation}

where $T_e^{agent}$ and $T_e^{theme}$ are the sets of triples where $e$ appears as Agent and Theme respectively.

\subsection{Validation}

We validated the pipeline through:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Manual triple verification}: Two coders verified 500 randomly sampled triples for correct extraction (agreement: 91.2\%)
    \item \textbf{Entity classification accuracy}: Manual verification of 300 entity classifications (accuracy: 87.3\%)
    \item \textbf{Lexicon coverage}: 58.0\% of extracted verbs matched the lexicon (comparable to prior applications)
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{High Power Differential: Climate-KIC and InnoEnergy}

Climate-KIC and InnoEnergy show the highest power scores, reflecting discourse emphasizing \textit{transformation} and \textit{system change}.\sidenote{Climate and energy transitions involve confronting incumbent systems, which may explain why discourse emphasizes power differentials.} Characteristic verb patterns:

\begin{itemize}
    \item ``[KIC] \textit{transforms} industries''
    \item ``Entrepreneurs \textit{disrupt} incumbents''
    \item ``Innovation \textit{replaces} fossil fuels''
\end{itemize}

These verbs encode power \textit{over}---the capacity to change others, often against their interests.

\subsubsection{Lower Power Differential: EIT Digital and EIT Health}

EIT Digital and EIT Health show lower power scores, reflecting discourse emphasizing \textit{enabling} and \textit{connecting}.\marginnote{Digital and health innovation may be framed more as enhancement than transformation, reducing confrontational power language.} Characteristic verb patterns:

\begin{itemize}
    \item ``[KIC] \textit{connects} partners''
    \item ``Technology \textit{enables} new possibilities''
    \item ``Entrepreneurs \textit{collaborate} with industry''
\end{itemize}

These verbs encode power \textit{with}---facilitation rather than domination.

\subsubsection{Culture \& Creativity: The Exception}

EIT Culture \& Creativity shows the lowest power and agency scores.\sidenote{Cultural/creative sector discourse may resist hierarchical power framings more than technology-focused KICs.} This may reflect:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Newer KIC with less institutionalized discourse
    \item Creative industries' emphasis on collaboration over disruption
    \item Different stakeholder expectations about power relations
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion: Power Dynamics in Innovation Ecosystem Discourse}
\labsec{power:discussion}

\subsection{The Linguistic Construction of Innovation Hierarchies}

Our findings reveal systematic power hierarchies in EIT discourse.\marginnote{These linguistic patterns do not merely \textit{reflect} power relations but actively \textit{construct} them by naturalizing certain actor positions as agents and others as beneficiaries.} Innovation intermediaries (KICs) and innovation protagonists (entrepreneurs) are consistently positioned with high agency and power; societal beneficiaries (citizens, communities) and learning actors (students) are positioned as recipients rather than agents.

This linguistic structure naturalizes a particular model of innovation governance:\sidenote{The ``deficit model'' assumes innovation is delivered by experts to passive publics, contrasting with participatory models that position citizens as co-creators \cite{irwin2006changing}.}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Expert-driven}: Innovation is produced by specialized actors (entrepreneurs, researchers) and distributed by intermediaries (KICs)
    \item \textbf{Top-down}: Power flows from funders to funded, from transformers to transformed
    \item \textbf{Beneficiary-passive}: Society receives benefits rather than shaping directions
\end{enumerate}

This model contrasts with participatory or democratic approaches to innovation governance that position citizens as co-creators of innovation directions \cite{stilgoe2013developing}.

\subsection{Implications for Sustainability Transitions}

The power patterns we document have implications for understanding who benefits from sustainability transitions.\marginnote{If transitions reproduce rather than transform power relations, they may deliver ``green growth'' for incumbents rather than transformative change for marginalized communities \cite{tyfield2012transition}.}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Voice and representation}: Actors positioned as low-agency beneficiaries have limited discursive resources for articulating alternative transition pathways

    \item \textbf{Accountability direction}: Power flows from KICs to entrepreneurs to society---but accountability mechanisms (what society can demand of KICs) are linguistically weak

    \item \textbf{Transition politics}: By depoliticizing transitions as technical innovation processes, power discourse may obscure conflicts over transition directions
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Cross-KIC Variation and Sectoral Logics}

Variation across KICs reveals how sectoral contexts shape power discourse:\sidenote{Climate and energy transitions may require more confrontational framing (vs. incumbents); digital and health innovation may be framed as complementary enhancement.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Transformation discourse} (Climate-KIC, InnoEnergy): High power differentials, verbs of replacement and disruption---appropriate for sectors confronting incumbent interests

    \item \textbf{Enablement discourse} (EIT Digital, EIT Health): Lower power differentials, verbs of connection and support---appropriate for sectors emphasizing collaboration

    \item \textbf{Creative discourse} (Culture \& Creativity): Lowest power differentials, resisting hierarchical framings---appropriate for sectors valuing horizontal collaboration
\end{itemize}

These variations suggest that power discourse is not merely institutional boilerplate but responds to perceived sectoral dynamics.

\subsection{Methodological Contributions}

Our analysis demonstrates the value of verb-based connotation frame analysis for studying innovation discourse:\marginnote{Verb analysis captures implicit power claims that explicit statements about ``empowerment'' or ``participation'' may obscure.}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Systematic}: Enables corpus-scale analysis of power construction
    \item \textbf{Interpretable}: Verb patterns provide concrete evidence for power claims
    \item \textbf{Comparative}: Enables cross-organization and cross-sector comparison
    \item \textbf{Implicit}: Captures taken-for-granted assumptions invisible in explicit statements
\end{enumerate}

The approach complements qualitative discourse analysis by providing systematic evidence while preserving attention to linguistic detail.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations qualify our findings:\sidenote{Connotation frames are context-independent lexical resources; actual power connotations may vary by context in ways the lexicon does not capture.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lexicon coverage}: 42\% of extracted verbs did not match the lexicon, potentially missing domain-specific power constructions

    \item \textbf{Context independence}: Connotation scores are lexical averages; actual connotations may vary by sentence context

    \item \textbf{English only}: Analysis limited to English-language communications; power constructions may differ in other languages

    \item \textbf{Production not reception}: We analyze produced discourse, not how audiences interpret power framings
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\labsec{power:conclusion}

This chapter applied verb-based connotation frame analysis to examine power and agency dynamics in EIT Knowledge and Innovation Community discourse.\marginnote{By examining \textit{how} innovation actors are linguistically positioned, we reveal the implicit power structures that explicit mission statements may obscure.} Extracting 64,485 Agent-Verb-Theme triples from 9,423 documents, we measured how different actor categories are positioned in terms of power and agency.

\subsection{Summary of Findings}

Our analysis revealed:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Systematic power hierarchy}: KICs and entrepreneurs are positioned with high agency and power; students and society are positioned as passive beneficiaries

    \item \textbf{Cross-KIC variation}: Climate/energy KICs show highest power differentials (transformation discourse); digital/health KICs show lower differentials (enablement discourse)

    \item \textbf{Characteristic verb patterns}: High-power actors ``support,'' ``accelerate,'' ``transform''; low-power actors ``receive,'' ``benefit,'' ``need''

    \item \textbf{Asymmetric dyads}: KICs act upon entrepreneurs, students, and society; reverse relations are rare and linguistically weak
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Implications}

For innovation policy, our findings suggest attention to:

\begin{itemize}
    \item How discourse constructs some actors as agents and others as beneficiaries
    \item Whether participatory rhetoric (``empowerment,'' ``co-creation'') is matched by participatory verb patterns
    \item How sectoral framings (transformation vs. enablement) shape expectations about transition governance
\end{itemize}

For sustainability transitions research, our approach offers a systematic method for examining power constructions at scale while preserving interpretability.

\subsection{Future Research}

Future research might:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Extend lexicons}: Develop domain-specific connotation frames for innovation/sustainability discourse
    \item \textbf{Longitudinal analysis}: Track how power constructions evolve as KICs mature
    \item \textbf{Comparative analysis}: Compare EIT discourse with other innovation intermediary types
    \item \textbf{Reception studies}: Examine how stakeholders interpret power framings
    \item \textbf{Intervention studies}: Test whether alternative verb choices shift power perceptions
\end{enumerate}
