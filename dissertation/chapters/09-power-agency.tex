\section{Introduction: Who Does What to Whom in Innovation Ecosystems?}[Introduction]
\labsec{power:intro}

How do innovation intermediaries linguistically construct power relations among ecosystem actors?\marginnote{Power relations in innovation ecosystems shape resource allocation, decision-making authority, and whose voices are heard in sustainability transitions \cite{avelino2017power}.} When the European Institute of Innovation and Technology (EIT) describes how ``Climate-KIC \textit{accelerates} startups'' or how ``entrepreneurs \textit{transform} industries,'' these verb choices encode implicit claims about who holds power, who exercises agency, and who is acted upon. Such linguistic framings are not neutral descriptions but active constructions that shape how stakeholders understand their roles within innovation ecosystems and, ultimately, who benefits from sustainability transitions.

This chapter applies verb-based connotation frame analysis to examine power and agency dynamics in EIT Knowledge and Innovation Community (KIC) communications.\sidenote{Connotation frames capture the implied social dynamics encoded in verb predicates---who has power over whom, who exercises agency, and how actors are valued \cite{rashkin2016connotation} \cite{sap2017connotation}.} Building on the RIVETER framework developed by Antoniak et al. \cite{antoniak2023riveter}, we extract Agent-Verb-Theme triples from EIT discourse to measure how different actors---entrepreneurs, students, researchers, corporations, policymakers, and the KICs themselves---are positioned in power hierarchies and attributed agency. This approach moves beyond analyzing \textit{what} is said to examine \textit{how} it is said: the grammatical and semantic structures through which social relations are encoded.

The EIT ecosystem provides an ideal setting for examining power dynamics in sustainability transitions discourse.\marginnote{The EIT's mandate to integrate education, research, and business brings together actors with historically different power positions and institutional logics.} The eight KICs---Climate-KIC, EIT Digital, InnoEnergy, EIT Health, RawMaterials, EIT Food, EIT Manufacturing, and EIT Urban Mobility---collectively address major societal challenges through innovation. Their communications construct narratives about transformation, disruption, and system change that necessarily position some actors as agents of change and others as objects of intervention. Understanding these constructions matters because they shape expectations, legitimacy, and ultimately the distribution of benefits from innovation-driven transitions.

Our analysis addresses three research questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{RQ1}: How are different actor types (entrepreneurs, students, researchers, corporations, KICs) positioned in terms of power and agency in EIT discourse?
    \item \textbf{RQ2}: How do power and agency framings vary across KICs addressing different sustainability challenges?
    \item \textbf{RQ3}: What verb patterns characterize high-power versus low-power actor positions, and what do these reveal about implicit assumptions in innovation ecosystem discourse?
\end{enumerate}

Our findings reveal systematic asymmetries in how EIT discourse constructs power relations. Institutional actors---the EIT itself, the European Commission, and specific KICs---are consistently framed with high agency and power; actor categories like ``Startups'' and ``Partners'' carry negative power scores and more frequently appear as objects of institutional action than as autonomous agents. ``Partners'' show a particularly striking pattern: high agency (0.621) combined with negative power ($-0.224$)---they are constructed as active but subordinate. \section{Background: Verb Predicates, Power, and Agency}[Background]
\labsec{power:background}

\subsection{Connotation Frames: Capturing Implied Social Dynamics}

Understanding who does what to whom has been central to linguistics since P\=a\d{n}ini's grammar in the 4th century BCE \cite{fillmore1976frame}.\marginnote{Frame semantics argues that understanding a sentence involves knowledge evoked by concepts in the sentence, not just denotational meaning.} Modern approaches to semantic role labeling categorize how words relate to verb predicates: the \textsc{Agent} causes an event; the \textsc{Theme} is most directly affected by it \cite{baker1998berkeley}. But beyond these structural roles, verbs carry \textit{connotative} meanings---implied social dynamics that shape interpretation.

Rashkin et al. \cite{rashkin2016connotation} introduced \textit{connotation frames} to model these implied meanings.\sidenote{Connotation frames were first applied to analyze political bias in news articles and have since been used across diverse domains including film, social media, textbooks, and Wikipedia \cite{antoniak2023riveter}.} For any verb predicate $v$ with Agent $a$ and Theme $t$, connotation frames capture:

\begin{description}
    \item[Effect] Whether $v$ has positive or negative effect on $a$ or $t$
    \item[Value] Whether $a$ or $t$ are presupposed to have value
    \item[State] The likely mental state of $a$ or $t$ as result of $v$
    \item[Perspective] Sentiment relations between $a$ and $t$, and how writer/reader perceive them
\end{description}

Sap et al. \cite{sap2017connotation} extended this framework with \textit{power} and \textit{agency} dimensions:

\begin{description}
    \item[Power] The power differential between Agent and Theme. When someone ``\textit{traps}'' another, the Agent has power over the Theme.
    \item[Agency] Whether the Agent is decisive and can act upon their environment. ``\textit{Slicing}'' connotes high agency; ``\textit{obeying}'' connotes low agency.
\end{description}

We extend this line of research to innovation ecosystem discourse, examining how EIT communications construct power relations among ecosystem actors.

\subsection{Power in Sustainability Transitions}

Sustainability transitions research has increasingly recognized power as central to understanding how---and for whom---transitions unfold \cite{avelino2017power,meadowcroft2009politics,geels2014regime}.\marginnote{``Transitions are not just technical or economic processes; they are deeply political processes involving struggles over the direction of change'' \cite{meadowcroft2009politics}.} Transitions involve not merely technological substitution but reconfigurations of actor relationships, institutional arrangements, and distributions of costs and benefits.

\cite{avelino2017power} distinguish multiple forms of power relevant to transitions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reinforcive power}: Capacity to reinforce existing structures
    \item \textbf{Innovative power}: Capacity to create new resources and structures
    \item \textbf{Transformative power}: Capacity to fundamentally alter distributions
\end{itemize}

Innovation intermediaries like EIT KICs occupy ambiguous positions in these power dynamics.\sidenote{Intermediaries may either reinforce existing power structures by privileging incumbent actors or enable transformative power by supporting challengers and outsiders.} They possess resources (funding, networks, legitimacy) that they distribute to selected actors; their selections implicitly construct hierarchies of worthiness and capability. How intermediaries \textit{linguistically} construct these relations---who is framed as having power and agency---both reflects and shapes the actual distribution of transition benefits.

\subsection{Linguistic Framing of Innovation}

Research on innovation discourse has documented how language shapes perceptions of technological change \cite{lakoff1980metaphors}.\marginnote{Innovation discourse typically employs heroic narratives featuring entrepreneurial protagonists who overcome obstacles to deliver progress \cite{pfotenhauer2019politics}.} Terms like ``disruption,'' ``transformation,'' and ``revolution'' carry implicit assumptions about the desirability and inevitability of change \cite{vinsel2021innovation,godin2015innovation}. Such framings privilege certain actors (disruptors, innovators, entrepreneurs) while marginalizing others (incumbents, traditional practitioners, affected communities).

Critical discourse analysis has examined how innovation policy constructs subject positions:\sidenote{Subject positions are the identities and roles that discourse makes available to actors---who can speak, act, and be heard \cite{fairclough2013critical}.}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \cite{jessop2013cultural} analyzes how ``knowledge-based economy'' discourse positions knowledge workers as agents and others as objects of restructuring
    \item \cite{tyfield2012transition} examines how ``green growth'' narratives maintain power asymmetries while appearing transformative
    \item \cite{swyngedouw2010impossible} critiques how sustainability discourse depoliticizes by framing environmental challenges as technical rather than political
\end{itemize}

Our approach complements this qualitative tradition with systematic, corpus-based analysis of verb connotations---enabling examination of implicit power constructions at scale while preserving interpretability.

\section{Methods: Extracting Power and Agency from EIT Discourse}[Methods]
\labsec{power:methods}

\subsection{Overview: The RIVETER Pipeline}

We adapt the RIVETER framework \cite{antoniak2023riveter} for analysis of EIT communications.\marginnote{RIVETER provides a complete pipeline for analyzing verb connotations associated with entities, including parsing, entity recognition, coreference resolution, and lexicon matching.} The pipeline proceeds in five stages:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Document parsing}: Tokenization, sentence segmentation, part-of-speech tagging
    \item \textbf{Entity recognition}: Identification of people, organizations, and actor categories
    \item \textbf{Coreference resolution}: Linking pronouns and references to canonical entities
    \item \textbf{Triple extraction}: Identifying Agent-Verb-Theme relations via dependency parsing
    \item \textbf{Entity quality filtering}: Removing pronoun/noise actors, NER whitelist validation, name normalization
    \item \textbf{Lexicon matching}: Scoring verbs for power and agency dimensions
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Corpus Construction}

We constructed a corpus from EIT KIC public communications spanning 2015--2024:\sidenote{The corpus emphasizes strategic and promotional communications where power framing is most salient; technical reports and financial documents were excluded.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Website content}: Main pages, programme descriptions, news articles
    \item \textbf{Strategic documents}: Annual reports, impact reports, strategy documents
    \item \textbf{Promotional materials}: Brochures, press releases, social media posts
    \item \textbf{Ecosystem narratives}: Startup profiles, alumni stories, partner descriptions
\end{itemize}

Table \ref{tab:corpus-overview-power} summarizes the corpus. After preprocessing (removing boilerplate, deduplication), the corpus comprises 1,273 documents containing 31,192 sentences. Raw triple extraction yielded 23,781 Agent-Verb-Theme relations; after a three-stage entity quality pipeline---removing pronoun and noise actors (4,599), filtering non-entity actors against a spaCy NER whitelist (9,459), and normalizing entity names---9,723 clean relations spanning 795 unique verbs remained for analysis. Entity type classification identified 925 initial profiles, of which 813 are substantive entities after removing 69 noise tokens and 43 date/number artifacts.

\subsection{Entity Recognition and Classification}

Standard named entity recognition (NER) identifies specific people and organizations, but our research questions require analysis of \textit{actor categories}---classes of actors that recur across documents.\marginnote{Actor categories like ``entrepreneurs'' or ``students'' appear far more frequently than specific named individuals and are more analytically tractable for examining systematic power patterns.} We developed a custom entity recognition layer combining:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{spaCy NER}: For named entities (people, organizations)
    \item \textbf{Custom gazetteer}: For actor category terms
    \item \textbf{Coreference resolution}: For linking pronouns and references
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Coreference Resolution}

We employ fastcoref \parencite{otmazgin2023lingmess} for coreference resolution, linking pronouns and nominal references to their antecedents.\sidenote{Coreference resolution is essential because many power-relevant verbs appear with pronominal subjects or objects: ``They \textit{accelerate} startups''; ``We \textit{support} them.'' Pronoun actors that cannot be resolved are removed during quality filtering.} When coreference chains include both named entities and category terms, we assign the chain to the most specific category.

\subsection{Triple Extraction}

Using spaCy's dependency parser, we extract Agent-Verb-Theme triples:\marginnote{Dependency parsing identifies grammatical relations: \texttt{nsubj} (nominal subject, typically Agent) and \texttt{dobj} (direct object, typically Theme).}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Identify all verbs in the sentence
    \item Extract the \texttt{nsubj} (nominal subject) $\rightarrow$ Agent
    \item Extract the \texttt{dobj} (direct object) $\rightarrow$ Theme
    \item Lemmatize verb for lexicon matching
    \item Link Agent/Theme spans to entity categories
\end{enumerate}

Not all verbs have both Agent and Theme (intransitive verbs, passives); we retain triples with at least an Agent for agency analysis, requiring both for power analysis.

\subsection{Connotation Frame Lexicon}

We employ an expanded lexicon combining the power and agency annotations from \cite{sap2017connotation} (2,148 verbs) with transition-specific dimensions and buildup/breakdown classifications, yielding 2,354 annotated verbs.\sidenote{The base Sap et al. lexicon was extended using sentence-transformer embeddings (BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5) to classify additional verbs for transition-specific and buildup/breakdown dimensions.} The core dimensions are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Agency(a)}: Agent's agency level ($+1$, $0$, $-1$)
    \item \textbf{Power(a)}: Agent's power relative to Theme ($+1$, $0$, $-1$)
    \item \textbf{Power(t)}: Theme's power relative to Agent ($+1$, $0$, $-1$)
\end{itemize}

For each extracted triple, we look up the lemmatized verb and retrieve its connotation scores. Unmatched verbs (not in lexicon) are excluded from scoring.

\subsection{Score Aggregation}

For each actor category $e$, we aggregate scores across all triples where $e$ appears as Agent or Theme:\marginnote{Mean scores enable comparison across categories with different frequencies; we also report medians and distributions to capture heterogeneity.}

\begin{equation}
    \text{Agency}(e) = \frac{1}{|T_e^{agent}|} \sum_{(e, v, t) \in T_e^{agent}} \text{Agency}(v)
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \text{Power}_{agent}(e) = \frac{1}{|T_e^{agent}|} \sum_{(e, v, t) \in T_e^{agent}} \text{Power}_a(v)
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \text{Power}_{theme}(e) = \frac{1}{|T_e^{theme}|} \sum_{(a, v, e) \in T_e^{theme}} \text{Power}_t(v)
\end{equation}

where $T_e^{agent}$ and $T_e^{theme}$ are the sets of triples where $e$ appears as Agent and Theme respectively.

\subsection{Validation}

We validated the pipeline through multiple complementary strategies:\sidenote{\iconnews\ Data: \texttt{dissertation/data/rolebox-news/}}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Entity quality audit}: The three-stage quality pipeline removed all pronoun actors (zero remaining), reduced case duplicates to one (KONUX/Konux), and eliminated noise tokens from entity profiles
    \item \textbf{Lexicon coverage}: The expanded lexicon achieves 100\% coverage of the 795 unique corpus verbs (2,354 lexicon entries total), compared to typical coverage rates of 50--60\% with the base lexicon
    \item \textbf{NER whitelist validation}: A spaCy NER whitelist of 10,733 entities cross-validated actor extractions against independently recognized named entities
    \item \textbf{LLM entity type classification}: All 925 entity profiles were classified into semantic types using Claude Sonnet in batches of 50, yielding: Organization (177), Actor Type (178), Concept (193), Person (139), Program (32), Institution (29), Technology (23), Event (22), GPE (12), Location (8), with 69 flagged as noise and 43 as date/number artifacts
    \item \textbf{Relation quality validation}: A stratified sample of 300 Agent-Verb-Theme triples (100 KIC-stratified, 50 high-power verbs, 150 systematic) was assessed for correctness. Strict precision (fully correct triples) was 0.33; lenient precision (correct or partially correct) was 0.74, with the dominant error type being vague or generic agents (31\% of triples)---consistent with dependency-parsed relation extraction on heterogeneous web corpora where subject noun phrases often lack specificity
    \item \textbf{Gold standard comparison}: Claude-generated entity annotations for a stratified sample of 193 articles (4,767 entities, 1,664 relations) served as reference. Entity-level recall is limited (0.08) because the pipeline retains only entities appearing in verb relations, not all mentioned entities; precision (0.22) reflects the broader entity scope of LLM annotation
\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion: Power Dynamics in Innovation Ecosystem Discourse}[Discussion]
\labsec{power:discussion}

\subsection{The Linguistic Construction of Innovation Hierarchies}

Our findings reveal systematic power hierarchies in EIT discourse.\marginnote{These linguistic patterns do not merely \textit{reflect} power relations but actively \textit{construct} them by naturalizing certain actor positions as agents and others as beneficiaries.} Institutional actors (KICs, the Commission) are consistently positioned with high agency and power; individual actor categories (entrepreneurs, people) are positioned with near-zero power and frequently as objects of institutional action rather than autonomous agents.

This linguistic structure naturalizes a particular model of innovation governance:\sidenote{The ``deficit model'' assumes innovation is delivered by experts to passive publics, contrasting with participatory models that position citizens as co-creators \cite{irwin2006changing}.}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Expert-driven}: Innovation is produced by specialized actors (entrepreneurs, researchers) and distributed by intermediaries (KICs)
    \item \textbf{Top-down}: Power flows from funders to funded, from transformers to transformed
    \item \textbf{Beneficiary-passive}: Society receives benefits rather than shaping directions
\end{enumerate}

This model contrasts with participatory or democratic approaches to innovation governance that position citizens as co-creators of innovation directions \cite{stilgoe2013developing}.

\subsection{Implications for Sustainability Transitions}

The power patterns we document have implications for understanding who benefits from sustainability transitions.\marginnote{If transitions reproduce rather than transform power relations, they may deliver ``green growth'' for incumbents rather than transformative change for marginalized communities \cite{tyfield2012transition}.}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Voice and representation}: Actors positioned as low-agency beneficiaries have limited discursive resources for articulating alternative transition pathways

    \item \textbf{Accountability direction}: Power flows from KICs to entrepreneurs to society---but accountability mechanisms (what society can demand of KICs) are linguistically weak

    \item \textbf{Transition politics}: By depoliticizing transitions as technical innovation processes, power discourse may obscure conflicts over transition directions
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Cross-KIC Variation and Sectoral Logics}

Variation across KICs reveals how sectoral contexts shape power discourse:\sidenote{Climate and energy transitions may require more confrontational framing (vs. incumbents); digital and health innovation may be framed as complementary enhancement.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High power} (Urban Mobility, Health, Food): Strongest power framing (0.361--0.368) reflecting institutional authority over complex regulatory, supply-chain, and urban domains

    \item \textbf{High buildup} (Health, InnoEnergy, RawMaterials): Highest constructive framing (0.118--0.148), emphasizing building new infrastructure and systems

    \item \textbf{Emerging discourse} (Manufacturing, Urban Mobility): Lower buildup scores (0.062--0.063), reflecting newer KICs with still-developing institutional identities
\end{itemize}

The relatively narrow cross-KIC power variation (range: 0.27--0.37) suggests a shared institutional discourse template, with differentiation primarily in agency and buildup framing.

\subsection{Methodological Contributions}

Our analysis demonstrates the value of verb-based connotation frame analysis for studying innovation discourse:\marginnote{Verb analysis captures implicit power claims that explicit statements about ``empowerment'' or ``participation'' may obscure.}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Systematic}: Enables corpus-scale analysis of power construction
    \item \textbf{Interpretable}: Verb patterns provide concrete evidence for power claims
    \item \textbf{Comparative}: Enables cross-organization and cross-sector comparison
    \item \textbf{Implicit}: Captures taken-for-granted assumptions invisible in explicit statements
\end{enumerate}

The approach complements qualitative discourse analysis by providing systematic evidence while preserving attention to linguistic detail.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations qualify our findings:\sidenote{Connotation frames are context-independent lexical resources; actual power connotations may vary by context in ways the lexicon does not capture.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lexicon granularity}: While the expanded lexicon achieves full verb coverage, the Sap et al. scores are categorical ($\{-1, 0, +1\}$) rather than continuous, potentially flattening nuanced power differences between verbs

    \item \textbf{Context independence}: Connotation scores are lexical averages; actual connotations may vary by sentence context

    \item \textbf{English only}: Analysis limited to English-language communications; power constructions may differ in other languages

    \item \textbf{Production not reception}: We analyze produced discourse, not how audiences interpret power framings
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Design Knowledge Contribution}
\labsec{power:designknowledge}

Beyond its empirical scope, this chapter contributes three transferable design principles for cartographic workflows that extract power and agency signals from institutional discourse at scale:

\begin{description}
    \item[DP-N1: Embedding-extended lexicon for full corpus coverage.] Augmenting the Sap et al.\ connotation frame lexicon (2,148 verbs) with domain-specific entries identified through sentence-transformer cosine similarity to seed verbs demonstrates a reproducible approach to lexicon extension without requiring full manual annotation. Achieving 100\% coverage of corpus verbs---a hard requirement for unbiased entity-level scoring---validates the embedding-based extension as an operationalization of MDR-2 (theory-guided operationalization) that scales with corpus size.

    \item[DP-N2: Three-stage entity quality pipeline.] The sequential quality pipeline---pronoun and noise filtering, then NER whitelist validation, then LLM-assisted entity type classification (925 raw profiles reduced to 813 substantive entities)---demonstrates that entity recognition quality is as consequential as triple extraction quality for downstream power and agency scores. Investing in quality infrastructure before analysis, rather than post-hoc filtering, operationalizes MDR-3 (scalable automation) while preserving the interpretive validity of entity-level claims.

    \item[DP-N3: LLM--human hybrid annotation.] Using Claude for entity type classification combined with human spot-checking (100 sampled triples, 193 gold-standard articles) demonstrates a cost-efficient architecture for large-scale entity typing. This hybrid approach operationalizes MDR-3: fully manual annotation at 9,723-triple scale is infeasible; fully automated annotation without validation produces systematic errors. The hybrid achieves both coverage and quality.
\end{description}

Together, these principles demonstrate that journalistic corpora---produced without research intent---can be curated into precision-scored actor portraits that reveal the power architecture of innovation ecosystems invisible to self-report and network analysis alone.

\subsection{Implications}

For innovation policy, our findings suggest attention to:

\begin{itemize}
    \item How discourse constructs some actors as agents and others as beneficiaries
    \item Whether participatory rhetoric (``empowerment,'' ``co-creation'') is matched by participatory verb patterns
    \item How sectoral framings (transformation vs. enablement) shape expectations about transition governance
\end{itemize}

For sustainability transitions research, our approach offers a systematic method for examining power constructions at scale while preserving interpretability.

\subsection{Computational Infrastructure}
\labsec{power:infrastructure}

The analysis pipeline developed for this chapter is implemented as RIVETER v2 (\texttt{rolebox-news}), extending the Riveter framework \cite{antoniak2023riveter} with an expanded lexicon, entity quality filtering, and buildup/breakdown dimensions.\marginnote{RIVETER v2 extends the base Sap et al. lexicon (2,148 verbs) to 2,354 entries using sentence-transformer embeddings for transition and buildup/breakdown classification.} The package architecture reflects the analytical workflow:

\begin{description}
    \item[Expanded Lexicon] The \texttt{ExpandedLexicon} class combines three tiers: (1) Sap et al. power/agency scores for 2,148 verbs; (2) transition-specific dimensions via embedding similarity to seed verbs; (3) buildup/breakdown classification using seed-based cosine similarity. This achieves 100\% coverage of corpus verbs.

    \item[Extraction] The \texttt{RelationExtractor} module uses spaCy for dependency parsing to extract Agent-Verb-Theme triples, with passive voice handling (agent/theme role swapping), KIC-specific entity normalization, and a three-stage quality pipeline: pronoun/noise filtering, NER whitelist validation, and case normalization.

    \item[Scoring] The \texttt{EntityScorer} aggregates verb-level connotation scores to compute entity-level portrayal profiles with bootstrap confidence intervals (1,000 resamples).

    \item[LLM Enrichment] The \texttt{claude\_enrich\_entities} module uses Claude Sonnet for entity type classification (925 initial profiles in 15 batches, yielding 813 substantive entities), relation quality validation (100 sampled triples), and gold standard generation (193 articles with 4,767 entity annotations).

    \item[Topic Modeling] BERTopic with BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5 embeddings, UMAP dimensionality reduction, and HDBSCAN clustering identifies 8 thematic topics across the corpus.
\end{description}

\noindent The interactive RIVETER-X workflow interface provides screens for corpus management, lexicon inspection, entity profiling, verb contribution analysis, and actor cartography visualization. The complete implementation and demonstration notebook are available in the supplementary materials.\sidenote{Available at \texttt{dissertation/data/rolebox-news/}}

Understanding power dynamics in innovation discourse matters because language shapes possibilities.\marginnote{``The limits of my language mean the limits of my world'' (Wittgenstein). How we linguistically construct innovation actors shapes who can participate in shaping innovation directions.} How we linguistically construct innovation actors---who has agency, who holds power, who is acted upon---shapes expectations about who should participate in innovation governance and who should benefit from innovation-driven transitions. By making these constructions visible, we open space for alternative framings that position currently marginalized actors as agents in their own right.

\begin{summarybox}
\textbf{Key findings:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.1em]
\item \textbf{Power asymmetries}: Institutional actors (EIT, Commission, KICs) show high agency (0.67--0.86) and power (0.30--0.50); actor categories like ``Startups'' ($-0.14$) and ``Partners'' ($-0.22$) carry negative power scores
\item \textbf{Verb patterns}: High-power verbs \textit{publish}, \textit{develop}, \textit{provide}, \textit{launch} (200--122 occurrences); low-power verbs \textit{announce}, \textit{receive}, \textit{need} (126--92 occurrences)---encoding implicit hierarchies in grammatical structure
\item \textbf{Cross-KIC variation}: Moderate variation across KICs (power range 0.27--0.37), with Urban Mobility showing highest power and Health highest buildup framing
\item \textbf{Startups as subordinates}: ``Startups'' show negative power ($-0.139$) and appear as Themes in 22\% of mentions; ``Partners'' combine high agency with negative power---active but subordinate
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contribution to CAS theory}: Power analysis illuminates \textbf{P5} (hierarchy from accumulated advantage)---discursive centrality correlates with structural centrality---and \textbf{P13} (reinforcing feedback)---powerful actors are more likely to be attributed further power-enhancing verbs.
\end{summarybox}
