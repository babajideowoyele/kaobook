\chapter{Visual Materials: The Aesthetic Map}
\label{app:visual-materials}

\noindent\textit{Organizations materialize their roles through visual choices---color palettes, composition, imagery types, logo placement.
This appendix documents the bridging workflow that connects visual data
to role theory constructs, following the data--theory integration framework
of \textcite{maass2018data}.}

\bigskip


%===============================================================================
\section{The Bridging Problem}
%===============================================================================

Visual communication and role theory have developed in separate domains.
On the data side, computer vision, social semiotics, and visual culture
studies produce methods for analyzing imagery---CLIP embeddings, logo detection,
visual similarity clustering, compositional analysis.
On the theory side, role theory and the knowledge triangle framework specify
how actors claim and perform identities through symbolic presentation.
The challenge is bridging between these perspectives---using
visual patterns to operationalize role constructs, and using
role theory to guide what visual features to extract.

\Cref{fig:visual-bridging} shows how the \RoleBox{} visual pipeline
enacts this bridging workflow.
The upper path (\textit{from patterns to theory}) extracts implicit
patterns from visual data and maps them onto role constructs.
The lower path (\textit{from theory to data requirements}) uses role
theory to specify what visual features and dimensions to extract.
The pipeline itself---the image collection, embedding, clustering, and
logo detection machinery---sits at the intersection as bridging knowledge.

\label{sec:visual-patterns-theory}

yields role-relevant patterns.
The workflow proceeds through three stages:
data collection, pattern extraction, and theoretical mapping.

\subsection{Stage 2: Pattern Extraction}

Three families of patterns emerge from the visual corpus,
grounded in \textcite{kress2006reading}'s social semiotics framework.

\paragraph{Compositional patterns.}
Visual composition reveals organizational priorities through
spatial arrangement, salience, and framing.
CLIP embeddings capture semantic similarity---images with similar
subjects cluster together.
Dimensionality reduction (UMAP) reveals visual themes:
technology demonstrations, partnership events, infrastructure shots,
team portraits.\sidenote{%
    \textcite{kress2006reading} identify compositional metafunctions:
    information value (left--right, top--bottom positioning),
    salience (size, contrast, focus), and framing
    (connection or separation of elements).
    These compositional choices construct meaning.}

\paragraph{Interpersonal patterns.}
Gaze, social distance, and angle construct viewer relationships.
Direct gaze demands engagement (common in talent-focused imagery);
absence of human figures offers detachment (common in impact-focused imagery);
oblique angles suggest observation rather than involvement.\sidenote{%
    The interpersonal metafunction addresses \emph{how} images engage viewers.
    Strong interpersonal features (faces, gaze, close distance) create
    intimacy and demand; weak features create objectivity and offer.}

\paragraph{Symbolic patterns.}
Color palettes, logo placement, and visual metaphors signal identity.
Logo co-occurrence analysis constructs partnership networks:
logos appearing together indicate collaboration or shared platforms.
Color analysis reveals field conventions---sustainability actors favor
green and blue; technology actors favor blue and orange.

Three mappings deserve emphasis:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0.5cm, itemsep=0.3em]
\item[CLIP clusters $\rightarrow$ Visual registers.]
Semantic clustering reveals three dominant registers:
\emph{Partnership} (groups, collaboration, events),
\emph{Impact} (technology, infrastructure, products),
and \emph{Talent} (individuals, portraits, teams).
These registers correspond to the three knowledge triangle vertices:
partnership to education (human development),
impact to research (knowledge creation),
talent to business (innovation deployment).

\item[Interpersonal features $\rightarrow$ Engagement mode.]
Strong interpersonal features (direct gaze, close distance, frontal angle)
signal \emph{demand} and intimacy, typical of talent-recruitment messaging.
Weak interpersonal features (no gaze, distant framing, oblique angle)
signal \emph{offer} and objectivity, typical of impact-demonstration messaging.
The interpersonal dimension operationalizes how organizations
invite viewer identification.

\item[Logo co-occurrence $\rightarrow$ Alliance networks.]
Logo detection and co-occurrence analysis construct visual partnership networks
that complement hyperlink networks (\cref{app:websites}) and
mention networks (\cref{app:social-media}).
Visual co-presence indicates alliance claims not always made explicit in text.
\end{description}

\section{From Theory to Data Requirements}
\label{sec:visual-theory-data}
%===============================================================================

The lower path in \cref{fig:visual-bridging} runs in reverse:
role theory specifies what to look for in visual data.
This is the \emph{theory-to-data-requirements} pathway that
\textcite{maass2018data} identify as essential for bridging.

\paragraph{CAS theory requires multi-actor visual presence.}
Complex adaptive systems theory predicts that role emergence involves
coordination and mutual recognition.
This requirement guided the decision to extract logo co-occurrence patterns
and visual partnership markers---images function as coordination devices
that make alliances visible and legible.

\paragraph{Knowledge triangle theory requires register features.}
The knowledge triangle framework hypothesizes that organizations position
themselves along Education--Research--Business dimensions.
This guided the visual register coding framework:
Partnership register (collaboration, convening) signals education orientation;
Impact register (technology, infrastructure) signals research orientation;
Talent register (individual portraits) signals business orientation.

\paragraph{Social semiotics requires compositional coding.}
\textcite{kress2006reading}'s visual grammar specifies that meaning emerges
from compositional choices---information value, salience, framing.
This requirement guided the extraction of spatial features
(center vs. periphery, foreground vs. background, connected vs. separated)
to operationalize how organizations construct visual hierarchies.

\section{The Visual--Textual Gap}
\label{sec:visual-gap}
%===============================================================================

The most theoretically productive pattern is not a data finding
but a \textbf{bridging gap}: organizations whose websites emphasize
community impact and partnership (text claims) often feature
predominantly product shots and technology demonstrations (visual claims),
with minimal partnership or human-centered imagery.

This \emph{visual--textual gap} is not noise---it is signal.
It indicates aspirational claiming (textual positioning for sought roles),
visual conservatism (technology imagery as safe default),
audience differentiation (text for stakeholders, visuals for customers),
or authentic tension (struggling to visually represent abstract partnership work).

\begin{criticalbox}[The Gap as Bridge to Other Modalities]
The visual--textual gap cannot be resolved within visual data alone.
Resolving it requires triangulation:
Do news media photos show partnerships in practice (\cref{app:news-media})?
Do investment portfolios confirm technology focus (\cref{app:crunchbase})?
Do social media images show community engagement (\cref{app:social-media})?
Each resolution path connects this modality's workflow to the next,
creating the multimodal cartography that the dissertation argues for.
\end{criticalbox}

\section{Summary}
%===============================================================================

\begin{summarybox}
The visual materials modality produces the \textbf{aesthetic map}---how
organizations materialize their roles through visual choices.
The bridging workflow connects 9,500 images to role theory
via two paths: CLIP embeddings and compositional analysis map to
visual registers (Partnership, Impact, Talent), and logo co-occurrence
patterns map to alliance networks and field positioning.
The most productive finding is not within either path but at
their intersection: the \emph{visual--textual gap} that reveals
tensions between declared roles (website text) and aesthetic performance
(website imagery), motivating the multimodal triangulation developed
across appendices.
\end{summarybox}
