\chapter{Social Media: The Relational Map}
\label{app:social-media}

\noindent\textit{Organizations perform roles through digital interactions---who they engage,
who engages them, and what conversations they join.
This appendix documents the bridging workflow connecting social media data
to role theory constructs, revealing the relational sociotype as performed
rather than claimed or attributed.}

\bigskip


%===============================================================================
\section{The Bridging Problem}
%===============================================================================

Social media research and role theory speak different languages.
On the data side, network analysis of Twitter produces follow graphs,
retweet cascades, mention patterns, and hashtag communities---rich structural
signals about attention, endorsement, and conversation.
On the theory side, role theory specifies mechanisms like proselytizing,
convening, and strengthening \parencite{powell2017organizational}, and
predicts that roles emerge from relational patterns rather than declarations.
The challenge is bridging these perspectives: operationalizing field mechanisms
through mention networks, and using theory to guide what network patterns to seek.

\Cref{fig:social-bridging} shows how the \RoleBox{} social media pipeline
enacts this bridging workflow.
The upper path (\textit{from patterns to theory}) extracts network structures
and discourse patterns from Twitter data and maps them onto role constructs.
The lower path (\textit{from theory to data requirements}) uses field theory
to specify what relational signals and interaction types to capture.
The pipeline---collection via TweetBinder, mention network construction,
community detection, topic modeling---sits at the intersection as bridging knowledge.

\subsection{Stage 2: Pattern Extraction}

Three families of patterns emerge from collected Twitter data.

\paragraph{Network structures from mentions.}
The \textbf{mention network} treats actors as nodes and @mentions as directed edges.
Standard network metrics identify structural positions:

\tableminimal
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{2.5cm}p{4cm}p{5cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Metric} & \textbf{Interpretation} & \textbf{Role Signal} \\
\midrule
In-degree & Many others mention this actor & Perceived authority, opinion leader \\
Out-degree & Actor actively engages others & Networker, active diffuser \\
Betweenness & Bridges disconnected groups & Broker, intermediary \\
Reciprocity & Mutual mention patterns & Partnership, peer relationship \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\captionof{table}{Network centrality metrics and role interpretation}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Community boundaries from clustering.}
Louvain community detection on the mention network identifies
\textbf{sub-regions}---clusters of actors who mention each other more
frequently than expected by chance.
These sub-regions correspond to thematic communities or discourse coalitions
\parencite{benabdelkrim-opening-fields-2020}.
In the EIT ecosystem, detected communities align with formal KIC boundaries
(Climate-KIC, EIT Digital, InnoEnergy, RawMaterials), validating that
Twitter interactions mirror organizational structure.

\paragraph{Discourse patterns from topic modeling.}
BERTopic analysis reveals dominant themes in Twitter discourse.
Using BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5 sentence embeddings, UMAP dimensionality
reduction, and HDBSCAN clustering, we identify four major topics:

Four key mappings operationalize field mechanisms:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0.5cm, itemsep=0.3em]
\item[In-degree $\rightarrow$ Convening.]
Actors with high in-degree relative to their community size perform
\emph{convening}: they attract attention and are frequently mentioned
as reference points, signaling authority within their sub-region.

\item[Out-degree asymmetry $\rightarrow$ Proselytizing.]
Actors whose out-degree exceeds $2\times$ their in-degree perform
\emph{proselytizing}: they actively broadcast messages and engage
others more than being engaged themselves, signaling diffusion effort.

\item[Betweenness centrality $\rightarrow$ Interfacing.]
Actors with high betweenness, connecting otherwise disconnected
communities, perform \emph{interfacing}: they bridge sub-regions
and mediate information flow across field boundaries.

\item[Reciprocal mentions $\rightarrow$ Strengthening.]
Actors whose reciprocal mentions exceed 50\% of their in-degree
perform \emph{strengthening}: mutual engagement signals peer
relationships and community cohesion.
\end{description}

\begin{criticalbox}[Methodological Deviation]
The \textcite{benabdelkrim-opening-fields-2020} approach discovers field members
inductively through Twitter list co-memberships: external observers create lists,
and co-listed accounts become network nodes.
Our approach uses a direct seed search via TweetBinder, collecting all tweets
mentioning the nine official EIT KIC accounts.
This yields a mention network rather than a co-classification network.

The trade-off: we lose the inductiveness of field discovery (our boundary is
defined by who mentions EIT accounts), but gain richer temporal data and
engagement metrics (retweets, likes, conversation threads).
Community detection on the mention network still produces meaningful sub-regions,
as validated by triangulation with internal sociotypes (\cref{ch:rolefield}).
\end{criticalbox}

\section{From Theory to Data Requirements}
\label{sec:social-theory-data}
%===============================================================================

The lower path in \cref{fig:social-bridging} runs in reverse:
field theory specifies what to look for in Twitter data.
This is the \emph{theory-to-data-requirements} pathway.

\paragraph{Field theory requires community boundaries.}
Field theory predicts that actors organize into sub-regions with
distinct logics and practices \parencite{powell2017organizational}.
This requirement guided the decision to apply community detection
(Louvain algorithm) to the mention network, identifying clusters
that correspond to thematic communities or discourse coalitions.

\paragraph{CAS theory requires relational data.}
Complex adaptive systems theory predicts roles emerge from interactions,
not declarations \parencite{holland2012signals}.
This guided the focus on \emph{mention networks} (who engages whom)
over \emph{content analysis alone} (what actors say).
Mentions reveal relational performance---actual engagement patterns
rather than aspirational claims.

\paragraph{Mechanism theory requires multiplex networks.}
Powell et al.\ mechanisms (proselytizing, convening, strengthening)
each require different network features: degree distribution,
reciprocity, cross-community ties \parencite{powell2017organizational}.
This guided extraction of multiple network types from single-platform data:

\section{The Claim--Relation Gap}
\label{sec:social-gap}
%===============================================================================

The most theoretically productive finding is a \textbf{bridging gap}:
many organizations claim ``strong industry partnerships'' on their websites
(\cref{app:websites}) but show no Twitter engagement with those partners.

This \emph{claim--relation gap} is not noise---it is signal.
It indicates:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.3em]
\item \textbf{Aspirational claiming}: positioning for sought roles
\item \textbf{Private relationships}: partnerships enacted offline or in closed channels
\item \textbf{Platform preferences}: some sectors avoid Twitter
\item \textbf{Performance selectivity}: public Twitter vs. private interaction
\item \textbf{Temporal lag}: partnerships established but not yet publicly performed
\end{itemize}

\begin{criticalbox}[The Gap as Bridge to Other Modalities]
The claim--relation gap cannot be resolved within social media data alone.
Resolving it requires triangulation:

Do investment flows confirm the partnership (\cref{app:crunchbase})?
Do news media attribute the collaboration (\cref{app:news-media})?
Do visual materials show co-appearance at events (\cref{app:visual})?

Each resolution path connects this modality's workflow to the next,
creating the multimodal cartography that Chapter~\ref{ch:rolefield} deploys.
\end{criticalbox}

\section{Workflow Challenges}
\label{sec:social-challenges}
%===============================================================================

Social media analysis faces six methodological challenges,
each motivating cross-modal bridging:

\tableminimal
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{3cm}p{4cm}p{4cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Challenge} & \textbf{What it misses} & \textbf{Bridging solution} \\
\midrule
API access restrictions & Post-2023 Twitter data & Archive data, alternative platforms \\
Bot contamination & Authentic human signals & Bot detection, filter outliers \\
Platform ephemerality & Deleted tweets, private accounts & Triangulate with news, Wikipedia \\
Representation bias & Non-Twitter actors & Complement with LinkedIn, web \\
Performance vs. practice & Offline relationships & Cross-check with Crunchbase \\
Discourse vs. action & Talk without walk & Validate with news attribution \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\captionof{table}{Social media challenges and cross-modal bridges}
\end{center}

\paragraph{API access crisis.}
Twitter/X dramatically restricted API access in early 2023, making ongoing
collection prohibitively expensive for academic researchers.
Historical archives (2015--2020) remain accessible through services like
TweetBinder, but real-time monitoring now requires alternative platforms
or manual collection.

\paragraph{Bot contamination.}
Automated accounts distort network metrics and discourse patterns.
Studies find bots post more neutral-tone messages while humans add more
opinion \parencite{sustainability2024bots}.
Best practice: use bot-detection tools (Botometer) and either exclude
likely bots or analyze them separately.

\paragraph{Platform ephemerality.}
Tweets can be deleted, accounts go private, platforms change policies.
Reproducibility suffers when data sources disappear.
Mitigation: archive collected data immediately, share tweet IDs (not
full text) to comply with terms of service.

\paragraph{Representation bias.}
Not everyone is on Twitter. Demographic and sectoral skews mean Twitter
discourse may over-emphasize certain viewpoints or sectors (especially
digital natives, NGOs, activists) while under-representing others
(traditional industry, SMEs, research labs).
Cross-platform comparison (LinkedIn, institutional websites) provides
corrective perspective.

\paragraph{Performance vs. practice gap.}
Twitter shows public performance, not private relationships or offline
actions. High social media engagement does not guarantee substantive
collaboration; conversely, absence of Twitter interaction does not mean
absence of relationship.
Triangulation with investment data (\cref{app:crunchbase}) reveals
behind-the-scenes ties.

\paragraph{Discourse vs. action.}
High Twitter activity about an innovation does not guarantee real-world
implementation. Actors may talk more than act, or act without broadcasting.
News media coverage (\cref{app:news-media}) provides external validation
of claimed activities.


%===============================================================================
\section{Summary}
%===============================================================================

\begin{summarybox}
The social media modality produces the \textbf{relational map}---how
organizations perform roles through digital interactions.
The bridging workflow connects 268,309 tweets (146,989 unique) to
field theory via two paths: mention network patterns map to field
mechanisms (convening, proselytizing, interfacing, strengthening),
and community detection reveals discourse coalitions.

The most productive finding is not within either path but at their
intersection: the \emph{claim--relation gap} that motivates multimodal
triangulation with websites (claimed), news (attributed), and Crunchbase (enacted).

Key network findings: KICs exhibit predominantly \textbf{interfacing} patterns
(mentions across multiple communities), while partner organizations show
\textbf{conforming} behaviour (within-community mentions).
This validates that KICs perform boundary-spanning roles through observable
relational patterns, not merely self-description.
\end{summarybox}
