\chapter{Websites: The Declared Map}
\label{app:websites}

\noindent\textit{Organizations curate websites to project who they want to be seen as---their claimed sociotype.
This appendix documents the bridging workflow that connects website data
to role theory constructs, following the data--theory integration framework
of \textcite{maass2018data}.}

\bigskip


%===============================================================================
\section{The Bridging Problem}
%===============================================================================

Website data and role theory have developed largely in parallel.
On the data side, hyperlink network analysis and web mining produce
rich structural and textual patterns from organizational websites.
On the theory side, role theory, complex adaptive systems, and the
knowledge triangle framework specify what roles \emph{are}, how they emerge,
and how they relate to innovation system functions.
The challenge is bridging between these perspectives---using
computational patterns to operationalize theoretical constructs, and using
theoretical constructs to guide what patterns to seek.

\Cref{fig:websites-bridging} shows how the \RoleBox{} website pipeline
enacts this bridging workflow.
The upper path (\textit{from patterns to theory}) extracts implicit
patterns from web data and maps them onto role constructs.
The lower path (\textit{from theory to data requirements}) uses role
theory to specify what data dimensions and features to extract.
The pipeline itself---the crawling, parsing, embedding, and
clustering machinery---sits at the intersection as bridging knowledge.

\section{From Patterns to Theory}
\label{sec:websites-patterns-theory}

yields role-relevant patterns.
The workflow proceeds through three stages:
data collection, pattern extraction, and theoretical mapping.

\subsection{Stage 2: Pattern Extraction}

Two families of patterns emerge from the collected data.

\paragraph{Structural patterns from hyperlinks.}
Hyperlink Network Analysis treats organizations as nodes and hyperlinks
as directed edges.
Standard network metrics---in-degree, out-degree, betweenness
centrality, reciprocity---identify structural positions that correspond
to role archetypes:
high in-degree signals authority, high betweenness signals brokerage,
reciprocal links signal partnership.\footnote{%
    Park's Hyperlink Network Analysis (HNA) posits that online link
    patterns correspond to offline organizational ties.
    This aligns with actor-network theory's treatment of websites as
    socio-technical actants.}

\paragraph{Semantic patterns from text.}
Topic modeling (LDA, BERTopic) and TF-IDF analysis of website text
reveal vocabulary clusters, discourse coalitions, and thematic positioning.
A critical finding: \textbf{vocabulary convergence} across the ecosystem.
Terms like ``innovation,'' ``sustainable,'' and ``solutions'' appear
near-universally, carrying little distinctive information.
The analytical response: weight \emph{distinctive} vocabulary
(terms that differentiate actors) over \emph{common} vocabulary
(terms everyone uses).

Three mappings deserve emphasis:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0.5cm, itemsep=0.3em]
\item[Network position $\rightarrow$ Structural role.]
High in-degree maps to \emph{knowledge hub} or \emph{authority};
high betweenness to \emph{broker} or \emph{boundary spanner};
tight clustering to \emph{community of practice}.
These structural roles operationalize the CAS mechanisms
of \cref{ch:cas}: convening, proselytizing, strengthening.

\item[Vocabulary profile $\rightarrow$ Claimed sociotype.]
TF-IDF signatures distinguish actor types:
orchestrators emphasize ``ecosystem,'' ``platform,'' ``orchestrate'';
research anchors emphasize ``research,'' ``scientific,'' ``knowledge'';
innovation engines emphasize ``solution,'' ``develop,'' ``market.''
The vocabulary profile is the empirical fingerprint of the claimed sociotype.

\item[Knowledge triangle balance $\rightarrow$ Positioning.]
Constructing keyword dictionaries for Education, Research, and Business
enables systematic measurement of how organizations position themselves
relative to EIT's integrative mission---a direct operationalization of
\textcite{maassen2011knowledge}.
\end{description}

\section{From Theory to Data Requirements}
\label{sec:websites-theory-data}
%===============================================================================

The lower path in \cref{fig:websites-bridging} runs in reverse:
role theory specifies what to look for in website data.
This is the \emph{theory-to-data-requirements} pathway that
\textcite{maass2018data} identify as essential for bridging.

\paragraph{CAS theory requires relational data.}
Complex adaptive systems theory predicts that roles emerge from
interactions, not declarations.
This requirement guided the decision to extract hyperlink networks
(relational structure) alongside page text (declarations).
Websites give us one input to emergence---actors' \textbf{role claims}---but
emergence requires testing claims against responses.

\paragraph{Knowledge triangle theory requires vocabulary features.}
The knowledge triangle framework hypothesizes that organizations position
themselves along Education--Research--Business dimensions.
This guided the construction of domain-specific keyword dictionaries
and the extraction of page-type distributions
(About pages signal self-categorization; Partner pages signal relational claims;
Services pages signal functional positioning).

\paragraph{Role constellation theory requires multi-actor coverage.}
The theoretical expectation of \emph{constellations}---complementary roles
interacting together---required collecting data across the full
ecosystem (6,487 organizations) rather than sampling.
Near-universal coverage (94.7\%) makes websites the most
comprehensive single data source for ecosystem mapping.

\section{The Claim--Position Gap}
\label{sec:websites-gap}
%===============================================================================

The most theoretically productive pattern is not a data finding
but a \textbf{bridging gap}: many organizations use vocabulary
associated with intermediary roles
(``bridge,'' ``facilitate,'' ``connect'')
but few occupy high-betweenness bridging positions in the hyperlink network.

This \emph{claim--position gap} is not noise---it is signal.
It indicates aspirational claiming (positioning for sought roles),
legitimacy performance (strategic use of valued vocabulary),
invisible bridging (intermediation through non-web channels),
or temporal lag (evolved positions with outdated websites).

\begin{criticalbox}[The Gap as Bridge to Other Modalities]
The claim--position gap cannot be resolved within website data alone.
Resolving it requires triangulation:
Do news media attribute the bridging role (\cref{app:news-media})?
Do investment flows confirm the intermediary position (\cref{app:crunchbase})?
Do social media interactions show performed bridging (\cref{app:social-media})?
Each resolution path connects this modality's workflow to the next,
creating the multimodal cartography that the dissertation argues for.
\end{criticalbox}

%===============================================================================
\section{Summary}
%===============================================================================

\begin{summarybox}
The website modality produces the \textbf{declared map}---how organizations
claim their roles through curated web presence.
The bridging workflow connects 6,487 organizational websites to role theory
via two paths: hyperlink network patterns map to structural role positions,
and vocabulary profiles map to claimed sociotypes and knowledge triangle
positioning.
The most productive finding is not within either path but at
their intersection: the \emph{claim--position gap} that motivates
the multimodal triangulation developed in subsequent appendices.
\end{summarybox}
